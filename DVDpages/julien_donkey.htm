Julien Donkey-Boy/C+,B
New Line/1999/99/ANA 1.85

    After seeing Gummo and almost totally failing to connect to writer/direcotr Harmony Korine's work, I sat down to watch Julien Donkey-Boy with a healthy dose of cynicism in my pants. The pretentious Dogma certificate that precedes the opening credits of the film reminds one of the British stamp of content approval of 1940s films should come as a warning to most viewers. This film ain't gonna look too good. In fact, Korine's hand-held digital filming making is often nauseatingly  unsteady. Talk about grain. The grain elements in Julien Donkey-Boy are so large images almost appear composed of snow flakes. After a while, some of the compositions remind you of the pointillist work of Georges Seurat. Korine's sophamore film is an interesting mix of repugnance, emotion, and awkward grace.
    Korine based the raw, very raw, material for Julien Donkey-Boy, on his skitzophrenic Uncle Eddy.  The world surrounding Julien, the insane composition of his family, his wandering mind, the love within him, are all explored by Korine. The structure is pretty free form, but it does follow an intelligible arc, examining variious aspects of the lives of Julien and his family. The parade of characters and situations seem natural to the world Korine has created for Julien. The insane motion of the camera, the unusual perspectives, do successfully echo some semblance of derangement. If you can get through the barrier of the difficult cinematic choices, there are some surprisingly powerful emotional elements to Julien Donkey-Boy.

Julien and a young blind friend. © New Line 

     Ewen Bremner is pretty amazing as Julien. The performance reminds one a great deal of Geoffrey Rush's tour-de-force perfromance as in Shine, however this is much more raw stuff. I still find it hard to believe that there was an actor playing Julien. Bremner finds a remarkable consistency to the character.I can't imagine that this guy slept too well at night after spending all those hours under the skin of Julien. Werner Herzog, the individualistic German filmmaker (Fitzcarraldo, Aguirre: The Wrath of God) plays the father with horrifying tunnel vision. I cannot believe that some of the stories that Herzog's character relates were not improvised by the actor. Myabe he should share in teh writing credit. Or maybe I am wrong and Korine wrote such a powerful character that the actor disappeared beneath the script. Chloe Sevigny disappears under an unruly red wig as Julien's innocent sister Pearl.
      What's it all look like? I suppose it's true to Korine's intent. In that respect, it's an excellent transfer. It is, however, almost unwatchable for the most part. You have to go with the flow on this one. The all-pervasive grain, hidden cameras luring unsuspecting people into the filmmaking process sacrifice image for naturally unadorned performance, but it's all artifice after all, isn't it. The sound it inconsistent, but the Puccini aria that repeats itself against the recurring ice skating image becomes transendental in its beauty. Dialogue, though often delivered in difficult patterns by lead actor Ewen Bremner, is easy enough to understand. New Line includes a short Julien Donkey-Boy making of produced by Korine. It provides an insight intot he director's thought process and what he wanted to achieve with the film.
