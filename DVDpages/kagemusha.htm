Kagemusha/A
DVD Review

Kagemusha A, B
Japan/1980/Subtitles/Widescreen 1.7/Stereo/159 minutes/Directed by Akira Kurosawa/Starring Tatsuya Nakadai/Fox Video/25 Chaps/2 Discs/CLV/$69.98
Akira Kurosawa is recognized as one of the greatest practitioners of screen art and Kagemusha ranks with the best of his filmography. Kagemusha was conceived during a period in Kurosawa's career when he found it next to impossible to obtain financing for his proposed film ventures. There was five years between Kagemusha and Dersu Urzula and during this span Kurosawa virtually made Kagemusha on paper, creating scene after scene in colorful storyboarding. The film existed almost in totality in Kurosawa's vision before it went before the cameras. 
Visually stunning, from sweeping battle scenes to poignant moments of character discovery, Kagemusha is a tribute to the uncompromising determination of this great filmmaker. Kagemusha, which translates roughly to the shadow warrior, is an historical examination of the nascent unification of feudal Japan. It delves into the souls of men and the structure of power. In its first scenes it lays out one it most dominant themes, the dubious distinction between great and common men and how situations often dramatically change the course of lives. A thief becomes the double of a great clan leader and as he mimics the mettle of this warrior chief, it sets forth a course of unalterable change in him.
Tatsuya Nakadai is wonderful in the dual roles of Shingen, leader of the Takeda clan and his kagemusha. Nakadai's wide eyed face portrays a wrenching expression of pain and longing. It is ironic that Kurosawa wrote the role of Shingen/Kagemusha specifically with another actor in mind, Shinaro Katsu, star of the successful Zatoichi series about a blind samurai. After casting Katsu, on the first day of shooting the film, Kurosawa and Katsu had a confrontation essentially concerning rule of the set. Despite his fragile industry position and his recent struggle to obtain financing, Kurosawa dismissed the star on this first day and waited several months for Nakadai to fit the role into his schedule. The uncompromising attitude of Kurosawa comes alive in frame after frame of cinematic perfection.
The Kurosawa hallmarks of superb visual composition and stirring use of music are in full flower in Kagemusha. After the rigidly composed moments of the prologue, Kurosawa displays his amazing range following a messenger's kinetic descent down a multitude of steps bordered by samurai's in various repose. 
The battle scenes are spectacular and many of the images presage Kurosawa's visual storytelling in Ran some five years later. Given the proper chance, Kagemusha will invest itself into your blood. On either a dramatic or purely visceral level this is an extraordinary film achievement. 
Kagemusha is a most welcome addition to the list of Kurosawa titles on laser. It's widescreen imagery seems seldom compromised, although the beautiful Fox gatefold jacket proclaims a aspect ratio of 1.85:1 and I measured an approximate 1.7:1 screen aspect. There are some scenes that are soft in focus, but they are mostly restricted to dimly lit filming and are perhaps more a consequence of the original elements rather than a careless transfer. The film's complex colors are alive and distinct. The pressing is very clean. The score is richly realized in digital sound. The white English subtitles are displayed in the lower band of letterboxing so as not to interfere with the image. Variable aspect systems will not be able to fill their screen with image without lose of titles.
Fox has provided excellent notes on the jacket in the form of an essay discussing Kurosawa's career, as well as a note from George Lucas proclaiming his sense of honor to have been involved financially in helping bring Kagemusha to the screen. The disc production will in no way impede in the thorough delight in this major motion picture. This is the international release edition which runs 159 minutes, while the original cut runs 179 minutes.
