Little Fugitive,The(SE)A-,B+

Image/1953/80m/BW 1.33

     Really quite a remarkable film, an ode to a past world of working class glitter, The Little Fugitive should not be passed up. Too many home theater enthusiasts are immediately turned off by the simplicity of black and white images (Not really so simple, are they?) and short change themselves of experiencing divine movie watching. 

        

Joey collects bottle at Coney.©Kino

      Two young brothers living in working class Brooklyn play in the streets, hang out with the guys, and interact with each other like myriad brothers before them. Joey is seven and his big brother Lennie is ten. The mundane neighborhood summertime play takes on another dimension when Lennie and his older friends decide they want to get rid of Joey for a spell. They convince Joey that something terrible has happened during the course of their play. The younger boy is petrified by the ramifications of his act and runs away from the scene to a place whose wonders can make him forget almost anything: Coney Island. 
     Photographed and related from the point of view of the young boy, The Little Fugitive brings to life the grand energy of the bustling beach. Bodies laid out end-to-end present a picture of 1950s America.  The photography is so startling that it could actually be broken down into a volume of stills. 
     Young Richie Andrusco is perfectly natural under Engel’s direction. Perhaps it’s the naturalistic style of shooting that makes the first time actor so comfortable under the lens’ eye. He never did make another motion picture though. But above all, the camera is the star and Morris Engel does an amazing job of making his instrument sing the song of Coney Island. 
     I must relate how powerful this film was when seen through the impressionable eyes of youth. Once upon a time when I was somewhere between Joey and Lennie’s age, I ran away to Coney Island from my Queens home. You’d never guess what inspired this fabled destination. Fortunately I stopped at my grandmother’s apartment in Brooklyn to charm a few bucks from her for the amusements and hot dogs and before the long day and night had ended the police found me wandering the Coney boardwalks and speeded me home in a patrol car. 
     Would-be filmmakers can use The Little Fugitive as a study course in how to approach filmmaking. The love embellished on the celluloid is evident in every frame. As a bonus, Kino Video has packaged The Little Fugitive as a special edition with audio commentary by director Morris Engel. Remember, he’s looking back almost fifty years, but he still does a credible job examining the experience.  The elements for the DVD are in very good shape. Expect clean images with good contrast. Some of the dubbing sounds a bit false, but that’s not the fault of the DVD. Sound is clean and image is sharp enough to satisfy photography lovers. Not to be missed!
