Luzhin Defense (SE)/B-,B
Columbia/2000/106/ANAN 1.85:1

     Trying to analyze the mystery of chess master Alexander Luzhin using flashbacks to a tainted childhood of prodigal talent and painful conflict undermines the great beauty that wants to flourish in Marleen Gorris's The Luzhin Defense.  An adaptation of a Vladimir Nabokov Russian novella, the setting is a plush Italian resort in full 1920's bloom. A world  championship chess competition is about to begin. Luzhin is the chief contender to overthrow reigning champion Italian Turati. The excitement of the tournament attracts spa visitor Natalia Katkov who is vacationing with her mother. The mother, ever in search of a suitor for her daughter, latches onto to an attractive young Count, but the independent Natalia has other ideas. On the eve of the chess competition, Luzhin's former promoter arrives for an added element of intrigue.

Contenders meet on an oversized chess board. ©Columbia

     I like the chess world setting and the chess games are actually quite dynamic in their presentation. Even the brooding study of aberrant genius is very effective when it stays grounded within the beautiful resort setting.  The romance that flowers between Luzhin and another guest at the magnificent Italian resort is improbable, but you can go with it. In fact, it probably should have been expanded upon. There is one wonderful montage that intercuts the flowering romance with the ongoing chess tournament that works brilliantly. Ultimately, the slow pace of the film is exacerbated by the muted color and internal terror of the flashbacks. They suck the magical beauty from the film like a mad artist destroying o his own canvas. The writing is at times very beautiful, like a good European short story. The psychological stuff is certainly Nabokovian, but instead of adding layers of mystery, it reveals the mundane core of an onion.  There are other plot contrivances that are too farfetched and are destructive to the balance of the film. The slow pace sometimes works against it as well.
     The production is first class. The period is lushly captured in magnificent resort settings. Design details are never in doubt. Director Gorris is right at home in the fascinating and lively international world of high teas and life size chess boards, but she is at a loss under the pall of the dismal flashbacks.
     John Turturro does a great job of making Luzhin quite human. Nevertheless, the character is overwhelmingly pathetic. Emily Watson is an actress grounded in reality. She is never less than convincing in her attachment to Luzhin, but one can't help wonder where the attraction lies? Geraldine James is annoyingly on target as the loud Katkov mother, a harridan in fine clothes. Stuart Wilson is far too smarmy as promoter Valentinov. 
     The DVD has a rather inconsistent look. I watched on a new Sharp 16 x 9 DLP projector and I discount that some scenes do not play as well without the depth of CRT black level. A number of outdoor scenes appear far too washed out exposure to the sun perhaps owing to artistic filtering to capture a sense of another and another time. Overall, the colors are generally accurate . Sharpness is good enough to reveal the details of the handsome production design but rather less than penetrating. The Dolby Digital 5:1 surround captures the reserved excitement of the resort and the lush chords of Alexandre Desplat's score are treated with respect. Packaged as a special edition, Columbia includes director Gorris's audio commentary and a making of featurette.
