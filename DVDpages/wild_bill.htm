Wild Bill/C,
DVD Review

Wild Bill

C, B

US/1995/Color/Widescreen 1.85:1/Stereo Surround, AC-3/98 minutes/Directed by Walter Hill/Starring Jeff Bridges, Ellen Barkin/MGM-UA/34 Chaps/Theatrical Trailer /CLV/$34.98

 

            Walter Hill, who usually packs two six guns in his director’s garb, this time around seems to have chosen a beret as his signature clothing for Wild Bill. A western about the legend of Wild Bill Hickok, the episodic nature of the screenplay displaces any sense of dramatic tension. The many dream sequences and sense memories that haunt Hickok in video-like black and white images further diffuse the fire power of Wild Bill and clearly label this Hill attempt an “art” western without heart.

            The structure is framed around the present as an impending appointment with fate plays itself out around Hickok. To its credit, I’ve never seen the legendary “aces and eights” murder beefed up with so many details. By the time it comes the audience is just glad to get it over,  the power of the moment is watered down  by poor timing and the pathetic nature of the act.

            Another fine performance by Jeff Bridges is wasted in the dusty saloon setting of Wild Bill. Bridges makes an imposing Hickok, whether tending to the grooming of his mustache or blasting six shooters with both hands in every direction. The close-ups by director Hill on Bridges eyes suggest much more to the character, but the actor is never freed from the chains of the screenplay. Ellen Barkin isn’t given much to work with in the character of Calamity Jane and Diane Lane is well cast in the ideal of a beautiful haunting memory of Susannah Moore. Solid supporter James Gammon brings the bombast of legend to an entertaining role as California Joe.

            Maybe Hill should have gone the safe route here and made another linear action Western about the life and times of the fascinating Hickok. As is, Wild Bill is photographed very well and the editing is handled with precision but the film adds up to a mostly lifeless essay on the death of a robust Western legend.

            This is a rousing laser disc with a very impressive surround sound execution. The images appear to replicate the artistic intent of the filmmakers. It is at all times sharp and the colors have been rubbed with plenty of western mud to achieve a burnished look to the film. That, however, does not necessarily mean this is a beautiful disc to watch and the muted palette echoes the dull action.
