 A Horror Feast
By Stu Kobak

     When you are eight years old a horror movie can invade your every sleeping night.  In 1951 I went to the movies and saw The Thing, a melding of scifi and horror that left me with a year’s worth of nightmares. Yes, I suppose it may have had something to do with my brother, five years my senior, who took devious delight in reminding me just before I fell asleep that “The Thing” was going to get me.
     I just love to be scared out of my pants in a movie theater. It seldom happens. Let’s face it, adults don’t scare as easy as kids. Though the The Thing remains vivid in my memory (I last watched the original on laser several years ago,), if you think for a minute my wife’s reminder that The Thing was gonna get me could give me nightmares, you are greatly mistaken. Still, The Thing remains a terrific achievement in its direct, almost documentary approach to the arctic alien arrival and the ensuing battle to protect the Earth. There are horror films that I relish as an adult and for a variety of reasons they speak to me.
     There are many varieties int he horror genre. The Thing melds horror and scifi, just like Alien or more recently. Then there is horror blended with suspense. Poe's Tell tale heart is considered a horror tale, but the moment in George Sluizer's original The Vanishing is every bit as horrifying as any Poe moment, thought he build up is pure suspense.

Freaks

Few of us are old enough to remember the reality of the circus sideshow, also known as the freak show. Those who have seen one in heartless garish light exacerbated by the animal stench of circus waste will never forget the experience. Freaks, the 1929 classic horror film directed by Tod Browning brings it all back, albeit,  padded by layers of time and tempered by the humanity of its characters.
     Though Freaks is considered a classic of the horror genre, in truth it is no more than an old fashioned morality play. A midget falls in love with a trapeze artist. While she finds his infatuation hilarious, she marries him as an opportunity to inherit his wealth. When her attempt to poison the midget is uncovered, his comrades of the side show extract  a fitting justice. What distinguishes Freaks as horror is the denizens of the side show who play themselves. The armless, legless, distorted bodies of those cheated by a quirk of birth are horrifying, yet as the sixty-six minute film progresses and reaches its satisfying conclusion, we begin to see these characters not as freaks but as people, motivated by humanity and able to act in their limited way to provide a dramatically macabre crescendo for the film.
     I watched Freaks in the first laser version from MGM and there were significant markings from dirt and time on the transfer elements. The contrasts are decent and while there are passages of soft focus, the disc quality does not detract from the film’s pleasures. The mono sound is okay and the dialogue, except from  midget Harry Earles, is easily understood. The remastered Freaks improves on the image considerably.

  

The Haunting

 

            Fear finds entry mostly through the imagination. A late night shadow on the wall appears to be an intruder. A creaking floor board raises the hairs along the back of your neck. The unseen or the ill defined may provide more horror than the obvious.

            Robert Wise’s The Haunting is a grand example of less is more. Dr. John Markway secures permission to use Hill House, known for its ghostly and ghastly past,  as the site for an occult experiment, bringing together participants whose past experiences with the supernatural are likely to create a receptive bond with the mysteries of the house. From the story by economical writer Shirley Jackson, Wise uses language beautifully to establish character traits in his players. Hill House, the other main character of The Haunting, is a set designer’s delight. Every inch of the Panavision frame contains elements of the gothic decor that enriches the ambiance of Hugh Crane’s evil house.

            Wise uses every conceivable camera angle to create an edge and off balance center to The Haunting. Using  the wide angle lens up close he creates “hall of mirror” distortions. Wise, a director who cut his cinema teeth at the editor’s console,  is spectacular in creating the perfect rhythms for this horror movie. It is the incredible use of sound, though, that stands out as the  hallmark of The Haunting’s horror. The pounding of the house travels from the moviemaker’s imagination to join in sympathetic vibration to the thumping hearts of viewers.         

            Wise has the luxury of some excellent performers in making the chills of The Haunting come to screen life. Julie Harris, as fragile participant Eleanor Vance, delivers dialogue as if it was born of the fourth dimension with  as many crooked edges as Hill House itself. Claire Bloom effectively alternates between amorous and ice pick lesbian instincts as clairvoyant Theodora. Richard Johnson is solid in the role of Markway and Russ Tamblyn provides a lighter touch to Hill House as the rakish heir to the ghostly abode.

            The Haunting uses the 2.35:1 frame to elegant use. Wise builds oppressive claustrophobia within the rectangular shape of widescreen compositions. MGM-UA presents The Haunting with 36 chapters and excellent black and white contrast in this laserdisc edition. Made in 1963, the film runs 112 minutes. The rented copy of the disc I viewed had seen some rough handling and many finger prints,  placing added burden on the tracking capabilities of my Pioneer CLD-99. Perhaps a slight warp produced unwanted herringbone line artifacts. The mono sound is clean and does not fall short when called upon for story embellishment. Additionally, a very enjoyable theatrical trailer is provided for a charming dessert after the horror feast of The Haunting.

Kwaidan

 

            Join hands and sit around the darkened camp fire, for Kwaidan’s four tales of the nether world perfectly replicate the feeling of listening to a mysterious tale told in ominous tones by a storyteller relishing the induced  silence of his audience

            Adapted from the stories of Lafcadio Hearn, an Irishman transplanted to Japan by way of America and Ireland, Kwaidan communicates the spirit and mystery of that faraway land, yet manages to speak  eloquently in universal terms.

            The first story is called The Black Hair. A samurai falls on hard times; in order to better himself, he leaves his wife for the daughter of an influential man in another province. Though his status has increased immeasurably, he finds the new wife self-centered and he is haunted by the image of his first wife. After many years, he finally decides to return to his former home where he finds his new wife looking still young and beautiful. But tales of the supernatural don’t end like that, do they.

            Next is The Woman of the Snow, my favorite of the four. Collecting wood in a white winter forest, two men are overcome by the elements. Finding shelter in a hut, they are visited by a beautiful female apparition. She sucks the breadth of life from one man, but smitten by the  looks of the younger man, she spares him on the promise he will never reveal the circumstances of the fateful evening. Soon after an orphaned woman shows up at the home of the man where he lives with his mother and they fall in love, marry, have a fine family. One night, the silence of his years old promise is challenged. Tatsuya Nakadai, eyes ever expressive, is wonderful as the woodsman.

            In Hoichi, the Earless, a blind biwa player living at a monastery specializes in singing about the ultimate sea battle between the Heike and Genji clans. The visualization of the sea battle is highly stylized, evoking Kabuki and Noh drama painted in brightly contrasting hues. When Hoichi is visited by spirits of the battle the priests try to prevent his possession.

            In a Cup of Tea is the final supernatural quarter of Kwaidan. A samurai ladles himself a cup of cool tea but before he can drink it the face of another samurai appears in the liquid. After repeated attempts at exorcising the face, the samurai drinks the tea. Later that night while on guard duty the samurai is visited mysteriously by the  man from the cup of tea. The story plays itself in perfectly horrific liquid progression. 
            Deliberately composed in images related to the Kabuki theater by director Masaki Kobayashi, Kwaidan is direct in its approach, short on dialogue and brilliant on image. The musical score by Toru Takematsu is atonal in character and works almost like sound effects. It enhances the essence of the supernatural in strokes as precise as a master samurai’s blade.
            The source material for Kwaidan is excellent with only a sprinkling of age related marking on this laserdisc of the 1964 film. The  2:45 widescreen presentation is a  visual powerhouse. White English subtitles appear in the bottom black band of the letterboxed. The titles are a blessing considering the dialogue is rough and distorted. The 164 minute movie from Voyager’s Criterion Collection  has 33 chapters and the two discs are pressed CLV and enclosed in a handsome gatefold jacket.
            What makes one horror film work for some and not for others? Each of us develop unique own horror nerve endings. Horror is time sensitive and age sensitive. When Lon Chaney, Jr. does his transformation from man to werewolf in Abbott and Costello Meet Frankenstein, Universal’s first  great  A & C horror spoof, it’s enough to frighten many young viewers out of the room and to an early bed time. What’s most horrifying to me is watching children clawed at by the clutches of the occult. What’s most horrifying for you?
