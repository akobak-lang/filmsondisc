     Celluloid Celestials

By Stu Kobak

     The ghost tale is a classic of storytelling tradition. It's easy to imagine scantily clad men arrayed around a night fire relating imaginary visits of less than worldly beings. Ghosts were often used by senior tribesmen to stimulate the fear of the unknown and to consolidate power. Ghosts, in cinematic terms, naturally, were used to bolster box office receipts. Fanciful tales of the night were brought to another life by the Hollywood imagination machine. Clever camera and printing  techniques provided filmmakers with a way of scaring the pants off audiences, and with clever timing of sudden ghosts appearances, more than a few moviegoers felt the hairs on the backs of their necks raise precipitously.

     There are ghost tales and there are ghost tales. Some features focus on the more frightening aspects of the other world. Dead folks haunting houses in which they met untimely deaths. Classics of this style of film, like The Haunting, the taut ghost story directed by Robert Wise, use many different paths to scare the daylights out of their audience. The Haunting used few special effects to create its other-worldly illusions, yet it was hair-raising and frightening, and if you watch it today, it stands the test of time. The other typical ghost treatment is the romantic melodrama, and this seems to be the most favored use of on-screen ghosts. A little charm, a premature death perhaps that is redeemed to a ghostly romance, typical ingredients of friendly ghost flicks.
