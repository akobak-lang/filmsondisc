Isn't it Romantic?

By Stu Kobak

     I can still hear Maurice Chevalier warbling the Rogers and Hart words in the classic Hollywood musical Love Me Tonight. And was it the night club scene in I Walk Alone, the first film starring Burt Lancaster and Kirk Douglas, that this song was sung against a brooding, noir backdrop?
        There’s romance and there’s romance. For young lovers holding hands in the moonlight, for gray-haired seniors caught in moments of the heart and wild men and women on the fringes of society, love is often the starting point for other themes. So let’s journey into the strange, wonderful, terrible world of love. 

A Walk In The Clouds

The very title shimmers with romance. Like the fragrance of a spring blossom, A Walk In The Clouds is unabashedly romantic. Director Alfonso Arau extracts every bit of elixir from his beautifully realized fantasy. With a more solid sense of reality than his wonderfully magic Like Water For Chocolate, he makes A Walk In The Clouds simpler -- a chance encounter on a bus leads from one world to another, from the bitter reality of returning war veterans to the rustic sweetness of wine country in full bloom.
        Keanu Reeves delivers an outstanding performance as Paul Sutton, the young chocolate salesman newly returned from service in World War II Europe. Reeves gives Sutton the requisite sturdiness of character and innocence to fashion an extremely appealing romantic leading man. Beautiful and sensual newcomer Aitana Sanchez-Gijon is thoroughly delicious opposite him, and the two ignite the vineyards with passion and purity. Anthony Quinn turns in a broad and delightful performance as Aragon clan grandfather Don Pedro, a lover of chocolates and love. Perhaps Giancarlo Giannini is a dram too sour as papa Alberto Aragon, but it doesn’t ruin the taste of the wine.
        Arau doesn’t lose an opportunity to coat his loving film with sugar crystals of romance. Blazing sunsets illuminate bucolic vineyards. Fanning the frost away is an excuse for amorous embraces. The first crush of the grapes is a festive purple dance laced with earthy sensuality. Even a night-time serenade isn’t too much for Arau’s romantic vision.
     The laserdisc is produced with a warmth complimenting the material. Variations of light bathing the vineyards at different moments are transferred with equal beauty, while the stereo surround serves big helpings of schmaltz. A Walk In The Clouds ($39.98) is required viewing for romantic laser lovers. It’s an unusual and welcome trip to the past viewed through the old-world eye of Alfonso Arau.

 

The Bridges Of Madison County

You can smell romance in the dust blown up by the tires cruising the lonelyfarm lane. A wholesome farm wife meets a photographer searching for the rightroad and their lives intersect in an old-fashioned yarn of impossible love.
        Meryl Streep and Clint Eastwood play Francesca Johnson and Robert Kincaid,chance lovers in a marvelously realized romantic interlude. Part of the fascination with The Bridges Of Madison County is watching these two pros atwork: that alone is almost romantic. Together they breathe an easy-going vitality into the dust-born story. Francesca’s Italian-American accent is a feast for Streep. The strength, the loneliness and the practicality of this wonderful farm woman is distilled by an actress of paramount skill.
        In some ways Eastwood is once more playing the man with no name. He comes into this strange place toting a camera instead of a gun and saves the day. Saving the day means giving Francesca even more strength to survive her life. Eastwood also directs the film with comfortable command. This guy knows his romance. The focus is on the actors, and while there are a number of beautiful scenes, he lets the camera delve deep into the characters rather than creating empty pictures. Working again with cinematographer Jack Green, he makes a film that is lit delicately. The camera is never showy.
        The screenplay is by Richard LaGravanese, who burst from nowhere to the top of the screenwriting world. Whether creating the wild and acid world of The Fisher King or an elegant adaptation of A Little Princess, LaGravanese has proven most sensitive. Sad to say, here he is less wonderful, nearly sinking the otherwise lovely Bridges with an intrusive flashback device to frame period scenes. It is not only dishonest, but thoroughly boring. However, he does well with the conventional aspects of the film.
        The same ease and professionalism that went into the direction of The Bridges Of Madison County ($39.98) is evident in this laserdisc production.  Every scene is sharply depicted. Color successfully captures a romantic light. Black levels are elegant; the contrast is very comfortable. The sound is first class. The Lennie Niehaus score is lovely, embellishing Eastwood’s fine directorial strokes. Delicate farm country sounds hang in the air, suspended alongside the romance of Francesa and Robert. The 2-disc set from Warner Home Video is 135 minutes and is presented in Dolby AC-3 Stereo.

 Carrington

Can this be love, this long-lived, strange affair of man-of-letters Lytton Strachey and painter Dora Carrington? Unconventional in the extreme, Carrington’s world features men with men, women with men, men with men and women -- and bottled up emotions frothing over. Set during the years between the world wars, this depiction of the avant-garde Bloomsbury crowd is tightly focused on character, though period and character cannot easily be separated, since these are the people who formed their time.
        While writer Christopher Hampton does a credible double as a first-time director, it is the two stars who shine. Dora Carrington gives Emma Thompson an intellectually stimulating woman to create on screen. She is most convincing. Carrington is a complex character and the actress, armed with the keen language of Hampton’s script, looks inward to find the core. Jonathan Pryce makes you believe he is Strachey. While the film may be titled Carrington, all the action centers around the magnetic power of this towering intellectual icon of Britain’s book set.
        This wonderful film becomes an almost unbearable laserdisc from Polygram. Carrington ($34.95) suffers from extreme jitter and frequent soft focus. Abysmal. The 122-minute film must be slightly time- compressed to fit on one disc, though -- perhaps distracted by the other flaws -- I didn’t notice anything.

 

Camille Claudel

Going even darker in the world of love, this blistering film chronicles the liaison between sculptors Auguste Rodin and Camille Claudel. Not only does this film examine the artist’s creative compulsion, it delves into its motivation and the results of stepping over the line onto the other side of passion. Director Bruno Nuytten records the creative-destructive process brilliantly, with an elegant look and intelligent, if unbearably sad, storytelling.
        Camille Claudel is the role of lifetime for the luminous Isabelle Adjani, who gives a completely unrestrained performance. She finds the very core of the artist and exposes the ravages of creation that destroyed the woman. There are moments of sublime beauty as Adjani indulges in free contact with her clay. Her possessive intensity is echoed in her devouring love-hate for Rodin. Gerard Depardieu is equally marvelous as Rodin. The usually explosive Depardieu finds a coolness in Rodin that borrows fire from the power of Adjani’s Claudel. These two magnificent performances mark this film with artistic achievement worthy of the subject matter, and the barrier between audience and screen evanesces.
        Bruno Nuytten, whose career as a cinematographer culminated with the sumptuous productions of Jean de Florette and Manon Of The Spring, makes his debut as a director with the 1988 Camille Claudel. He displays a sure hand with the actors; his camera expertise insures a spectacular look for his first film. Nuytten also co-wrote the screenplay and, though the film is dominated by acting, the writing provides a solid foundation of intelligence.  
        The production design creates the atmosphere of  turn-of-the-century Paris salons, where works of art are bathed in public light.
        The music is just fantastic. Extensive use of the surround channels creates a sonic expansiveness that compliments the powerful thematic material. Gabriel Yared’s score perfectly reflects the range of emotions.
        This is the second release of Camille Claudel on laser. Here the breadth and beauty of the widescreen images (presented in 2.35:1 aspect ratio) are a far cry from the chopped and cropped pan-and-scan version available several years ago. Nuytten’s difficult palette that combines ice blues with the earth colors of clay is well transferred on this laser. Rendered with a biting edge that heightens the reality of its main characters, the laserdisc fuses sound and image to portray the collapsing world of Camille Claudel to devastating effect. The 2-disc CLV (159 minutes) set is part of the Cinemadisc collection distributed by Image Entertainment ($49.95).

Bonnie And Clyde

Love at its darkest. At first glance, how can we even imagine this violent, stylistically beautiful action flick as a romance? After all, this was among the first graphically bloody Hollywood films. At its heart, however, it is pure romance, from the innocence of the lovers’ first meeting, which recalls the world of small-town girls desperate for something more than dust and chickens, to the poem Bonnie writes, "The Story of Bonnie and Clyde." The romance is punctuated, even created, by that violence that paves the lovers’ way to immortality.

        Dunaway was a stunner! No painted-screen creation, but a pure distillation of beauty and innocence with a fire inside turning her emotions to lava. As Bonnie, she dangles Clyde on a string, playing with his manhood, a siren moving him through his mad odyssey to a meeting with death. Beatty draws the perfect beat for Clyde. It’s an interesting performance. He displays a sensitivity that contrasts, without denying, that easy violence hanging as casually as a jacket from his shoulders.
       Arthur Penn directs the action with ferocity and grace. The pace is superb, moving naturally from scene to scene. The intimate scenes between Dunaway and Beatty are very strong and create a haunting reminder of the innocence of these characters during the most violent and explosive scenes. Penn guides his actors through the hail of bullets and rushing cars to unscathed performances. Gene Hackman makes his first great mark on the screen as Buck Barrow and Estelle Parsons’ ranting Blanche Barrow got under the skin of Academy audiences enough to garner her a Best Supporting Actress Award. Their marriage makes a powerful counterpoint to the love of Bonnie and Clyde.         Penn’s sterling work is embellished by the Academy Award winning cinematography of Burnett Guffey, a veteran of black-and-white noir. Guffey pulls out all the camera stops to capture the dust and grit of the Depression Era, while enhancing the violence to measured screen beauty.
        Warner Home Video turns in another fine example of a pristine laserdisc. Bonnie And Clyde ($34.98) gets the transfer it deserves. Detail is well defined, with no obvious blemishes. Presented in a 1.85:1 aspect ratio, the 112-minute disc has an excellent mono soundtrack. Clear dialogue, crisp music, perfect vision. A collector’s must.
     In these five films, we have traveled through the land of love, from the sunlight of young love painted in the warm colors of unabashed fantasy, to the beauty of a brief encounter, to the unconventional and irrepressibly bold embraces of the avant-garde to the maddening canvas of love and art and finally to love packaged in blood. Romance is everywhere, even in the darkest corners of life.
     Everybody wants romance. You can’t always get it where you want it so why not start a romantic laser spinning tales of the heart.. The five films perused here represent but a  spec in the universe of film romance. Romance packaged in blood, the unconventional and irrepressibly bold romance of the avant-garde, a beauty of a brief encounter, a fantasy painted in unabashedly warm colors or a maddening canvas of love and art. Go for it. Everybody loves a good romance. 
     The eclectic nature of the heart provides endless delight and fascination. Where does romance reside.? The impulses of love wend their way through different paths to each of us.
