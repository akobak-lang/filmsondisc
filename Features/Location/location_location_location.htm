Location, location, location

By Stuart J. Kobak

 

            There's an old adage in the real estate business: only three things are important about a piece of property, location, location,. location. This may not be true of films, but under certain circumstances location can have a more significant impact.

            Cinema can be enjoyed on many levels. The power generated by the magnetism of s star can be enough to stimulate and satisfy some viewers. When the level of  performance matches the astronomical sums paid today for the stamp of star power, then the experience can be enthralling. But performance alone doesn't make a good film. Let's not forget the words that weave the plot. Good writing, the skeleton of film magic, arriving seamlessly from the shiny Writer's top hat into the hands of a competent director almost always forms the basis for a good film. Set design too can make a major impact on the cinematic experience. Characters created by the writer, blocked and shaped by the director, can come to life in a world created for  the screen by an imaginative set designer. Superb cinematography can make a film  soar beyond its natural limits.

            On another level, location of a film can be a most enticing magnet to draw audiences to the world of  movies. For many of us, that first taste of the exotic was provided  within the darkened confines of cinema joy, where the old world streets of some European capital were projected in a incandescent wand of light onto a bigger than life screen that dominated all but the most peripheral view. A budding wanderlust began with the seed of a Roman Holiday seen through the innocent eyes of Audrey Hepburn, or perhaps Gene Kelly tripping the light fantastic through the streets of Paris in An American in Paris sparked a determination to follow his fabulous feet.

            In this sense, movies provided us a two dimensional gateway to the world. It shaped our views of the world. We became familiar with the Chinese peasants close up  through Paul Muni and Louise Rainer emoting in The Good Earth. The jungles of Africa enveloped us along with Stewart Granger and Deborah Kerr in King Solomon's Mines.

             Some films take the element of location beyond the simple fact that the story takes place there. The setting becomes an integral element of the plot, just as important as the actors or mechanics of the story. The location can take the shape of a major character, acting upon or interacting with the principal actors. This added dimension to the mere  passive setting of location can add dramatically to the allure of a film.

            David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia immediately conjures hot, dry images of the desert. The desert acts upon Lawrence; a bond laminated by the sheer power of heat inextricably joins Lawrence , played with icy sang-froid by Peter O'Toole, to the sands of his fate. Just as Lawrence must learn of ways of the desert, the movie-goer shares this experience.   

            Lawrence of Arabia magically builds its foundation for a movie classic on myriad desert images.  When the mirage-like image of Sheriff Ali, perfectly cast with Omar Sharif in his film debut, solidifies from plumes of desert heat, it is the physicality of the desert creating this instant myth of a desert warrior. Director Lean,  in one of film's most famous cuts, transforms the bright white flame of a match under             Lawrence's hand into the blazing rays of a desert sun.                             Rewatching the film, one can feel the grit of the desert coating the skin of the characters. Lawrence enters the desert a man and the swirling sands sculpt out a legend. I have contented myself to experience the desert in the cool of a viewing room, sharing the experiences of Lawrence through Peter O'Toole through the filter of the world of film.

            There are several current  laser versions of Lawrence of Arabia, all made from the same superb transfer of the reconstructed and restored film that was supervised by Robert Harris. Columbia-Tri-Star and Voyager both offer CLV editions, however only Voyager has chapter encoded the film, as well as including a pamphlet of reconstruction notes by Chapter compiled by Robert Harris.  As a bonus,   Voyager has also included some short promotional clips of the film, including some footage of the 1962 premiere. Ironically, the more expensive CAV  edition does not include the extras above, however, it has a number of production stills included on the final side of the four disc set. All of the versions are admirable in terms of video and audio quality.

                        Bridge on the River Kwai, David Lean's equally superb masterwork, captures the horror of a Japanese POW camp in the Burmese jungles. The film stars Alec Guinness as Colonel Nicholson, William Holden as American prisoner Shears, and Sessue Hayakawa as camp commandant Colonel Saito. While the Burmese location is perfectly captured by the Ceylon location shoot, it is not really the dense jungles surrounding the POW camp that attract the rapt attention of Lean's audience, but the fourth star of this film , the bridge.  constructed with the blood and determination of the British POWs.

            While the psychological battle between the Japanese commander and his British counterpart  is drawn in excruciatingly painful scenes of confrontation, the bridge becomes the pivotal character in the drama. As the spectacle of its construction in painstakingly detailed by the director, the bridge takes on a life of its own, dominating Colonel Nicholson where the threats and torture of Colonel Saito have failed. The bridge drives Nicholson beyond all logic in his attempt to survive the POW camp. The life of the bridge becomes more important than the survival of his men.  As the wooden struts are pieced together, Nicholson's own strength begins to build, obsessed with the mission to complete the bridge at all costs.

            Bridge on the River Kwai is a film for all time, however the only currently available laser disc, an old panned and scanned transfer from Columbia-Tri-Star, does not do justice to the film. Aside from the issue of aspect ratio mutilation, the colors are faded and fail to convey the overbearing power of the dense surrounding jungles adequately. Help is on the way, however, as Voyager is projecting a restored version of this cinematic treasure for some time next year. We  greedily await the coming renewed  release of this classic film.

            Lean represented another generation of filmmaker. With his passing  last year,  the world of cinema surely lost a giant. Lean's legacy has not only been left on the celluloid images created under his guidance, but is reflected in the influence of his work on current pretenders to his mantle.

            Roland Joffe has made a startling impact on the cinematic scene with only a few movies. Beginning with The Killing Fields and The Mission, location featured as prominent aspects of his films. His next film and arguably his only misstep, Fat Man and Little Boy, lacked an exotic locale. City of Joy, Joffe's fourth film and an unqualified success, again built on the foundation of location.

            City of Joy  is a journey into the heart of India's impoverished Calcutta ghettos. It is a journey of truth for Dr. Max Lowe, a search for the lost meaning of life. Patrick Swazee is very appealing in this role. Swayzee combines the right mixture of arrogance, innocence and passion to capture the portrait of this young doctor who by giving of himself finds himself.

            While City of Joy is a film about character,  and indeed features one of the more memorable film portraits in recent years, Hasari Pal, inspirationally played by Indian matinee idol Om Puri, Pal's character could only exist in the unique landscape of City of Joy.

            The details of the streets of Calcutta are as much a main character is this fine film as any of the flesh and blood principals. Joffe's camera fills the screen with the essence of the Indian streets as Hasari Pal pulls his rickshaw with soaring spirit along the sun-baked roads of bustling Calcutta. The soundtrack embellishes the images with a further sense of reality, shaping for the moviegoer a more complete impression of this exotic location. One's sense memory can almost detect the pungent, characteristic smells of the Indian culture, so full is the picture that director Joffe paints.

            It is the culture and landscape of India that impacts upon Max Lowe and stimulates the formulation of a new strength in his life. The people's of the ghetto form an entity greater than the individuals, the structure of the ghetto itself fusing with its human parts.

            Not only has Joffe presented an wonderful story of human behavior, he has also treated us to an experience of an exotic location that most will never have the joy of touching first hand. The weaving of this magic fabric, alternating strands of character and location, is a supreme accomplishment.

            Columbia Tri-Star has given City of Joy a first rate treatment. The transfer accurately presents the many colors of the Indian cityscape. There is some grain during the night scenes, but my memory of the film in theaters is that Joffe's use of low light required fast and grainier film stock for those scenes. The sound is very fine, from the powerful musical score to the sounds of the streets. Considering some of the formidable obstacles that Roland Joffe faced in making this film, a supplementary section would have provided exciting insights. Joffe is a fantastic speaker and hopefully his career will flourish and some future discs will include his commentary on the making of the motion picture.

            The Killing Fields is a spectacular film, never mind a debut for a fledgling director. The difficult location shoot in Thailand is blessed by fine scripting by Bruce Robinson and wonderfully resolved images from lens of cinematographer Chris Menges. Once again Joffe has the magic of an inspirational story as his ammunition, in this case survival against a land in upheaval and torment, Cambodia during the period preceding the occupation of Phnom Penh by the Kmer Rouge and their subsequent mad ravaging of the people. The detailing of the Cambodian(Thailand in reality)landscape and the city of Phnom Penh does not approach the richness with which City of Joy created, nevertheless this exotic locale draws a fascinating picture of a land erupting.

            Although the character of New York Times journalist Sidney Schanberg is nominally the star of The Killing Fields, it is more the story of Dith Pran, Schanberg's Cambodian aide, interpreter and friend. Pran's survival against great odds pits him against the irrational behavior of the Kmer Rouge. The depiction of his imprisonment makes a character of the scared land itself. Survival of the people is keyed to survival of the land, and Pran's heart-wrenching discovery of "the killing fields," is a haunting cinematic image.

            Joffe has kept tight control on all the ingredients in his virgin film. He has managed to exact performances from his actors that are as good or better than anything else they have done. Sam Waterston's lanky figure of Sidney is impressive enough to forget that he is acting. Dr. Haing S. Ngor, a Cambodian who plays Dith Pran, is a man uniquely close to the role, having lived through the turmoil depicted in the film. He gives a delicately accented performance that gave him an Oscar as Best Supporting Actor for the 1984 film.(There is no way that this was a supporting performance, yet here we have Hollywood bestowing on the major character of a film a Best Supporting  award.)Along with Ngor, Chris Menges won the Oscar for Best Cinematography and Jim Clark was honored for Best Film Editing on the film.  Other finely tuned acting support included John Malkovich as a daring photojournalist, Julian Sands as a British journalist, and Spalding Gray in a minor role as an American diplomat.

            Gray, imaginatively, created a entertaining monologue that was filmed by Jonathan Demme called Swimming to Cambodia, which focused on the fascination of the location shoot. Swimming might make an interesting companion piece for viewing after The Killing Fields.

            The location plays a major role in the success of The Killing Fields. A fascination with the faraway creates additional layers of pleasure for the audience.

            The Warner Home Video disc is filled with the sharply defined images of the camera of Chris Menges. The transfer, although at times somewhat grainy, is nevertheless good. Colors are stable. The soundtrack, including the excellent electronic score from Mike Oldfield, fares very well on the disc pressing.

            With the success of The Killing Fields, Joffe braced for the sophomore jinx in making another ambitious location film, The Mission. This time the Joffe team journeyed to the jungles of Brazil. Of the trio of "location" films, this is the least successful, although, the dense jungles and magnificent natural surroundings are used extensively to punctuate the film, this is more a study of character and faith. Yes, the surroundings do act upon the characters, but not to the same degree of importance as in the other two films.

            The Mission does fit the Joffe inspirational mold and it is fascinating to enjoy the contrasting acting of Jeremy Irons and Robert DeNiro. Irons is ice and DeNiro fire. Together they forge a bond that makes each give the ultimate sacrifice in keeping faith with the mountain Indians.

            The Cinematography of Chris Menges again was presented with the Academy Award for The Mission. Menges, guided by the perspicacious Joffe, captures the raw power of the environment surrounding the actors in The Mission. The rape of the Indians, amidst the stirring natural beauty, foreshadows the modern baring of the rain forests of today.

            The Warner disc does justice to Menges photography. Pristine images have been transferred from film to disc. The ferocity of the raging waters fill the surround channels perfectly.

            All of the films focusing on location in an unusual way are too numerous to examine.       

            Bertolucci's spectacular depiction of the life and times of Pun Yi in The Last Emperor must rank with the better efforts of featuring location. In this film Bertolucci gained access behind the walls of Peking's ancient "Forbidden City" to relate the story of change in China. Two domestic versions of The Last Emperor are available from Nelson Entertainment, one Pan and Scan in CLV while the other CAV set is widescreen. There is also a Japanese CLV pressing that has been transferred from the European version of The Last Emperor and runs some 50 minutes longer than the U.S.  version. Oddly, the pacing in the longer version is much better and actually seems shorter. It might be well worth trying to pick up.

            Sidney Pollack has ventured into the exotic with his fascinating film, The Yakuza. The film which stars Robert Mitchum as an American on a mission into the depths of the Japanese underworld, succeeds on a number of levels. As an action film, The Yakuza sparkles, but it is the knowledgeable examination of the subculture of the yakuza that takes this film beyond the ordinary, another example when the location adds a dimension of enhancement.

            For location to play a major part in film, one doesn't always have to travel far afield. Edward James Olmos has taken the viewer intimately into another world in his directoral debut, American Me. In the film, which also stars Olmos, life behind prison walls is detailed well enough to stand alone as a character acting on the protagonists.

            Can you imagine Dorothy without the land of Oz? Follow the black tar road on the road?  Can you separate Blade Runner from the remarkable set design that created a unique look at a possible future. Location is often an unsung star of the films we love.
