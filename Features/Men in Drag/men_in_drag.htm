Men in Drag

By Stu Kobak

     Men playing women's roles has a long history in theater, from Shakespeare to Peking Opera, boys have cavorted as girls. Hollywood has it's own tradition of drag, with both men and women disguising themselves

 

   "Bulls are bulls and roosters don't lay eggs," is the way Les explains life to Dorothy in front of a flickering fire in the hilarious cross-dressing movie Tootsie. Considering the way Les's heartstrings were plucked by Dorothy, his reaction to finding out Dorothy is really a man is quite restrained. It probably has something to do with the Hollywood profit mentality. The gracefully rotund Charles Durning gives Les more and Dustin Hoffman's Tootsie is a scream classic. One of the joys of Tootsie is that it really tries putting a man in a woman's skin, looking for female insights through a male psyche.

There's a fine line between appearing in drag and cross dressing. In Ed Wood Johnny Depp samples a bit of the ladies garb, but he never tries to pass himself off as anything but a daffy eccentric filmmaker with a fondness for alpacca sweaters and silky bras.

Comic's loved an opportunity to make fun of the opposite sex by usurping their identities. Bob Hope spent time in ladies garments in a few films, most notably in the Road to Rio. Hope as a Latin American bombshell is an explosion of laughs. Hope does a small drag bit as his own aunt in a Crosby fantasy in The Road to Morocco. Now, Bing, what were you thinking?

Bing finally got his revenge but Hope had no part in it. In the 1960 film High Time directed by Blake Edwards, Bing puts on too much make-up to convince anyone he remotely resembles Scarlett O'Hara. Vivien Leigh was rumored to have set fire to the set and Gable just passed away.

In Steve Buscemi's sophomore film, Animal Factory, just released on DVD, Mickey Rourke digs deep beyond his fighters biceps to come up with a prison drag queen interpretation. Strange casting, man. Mickey doesn't even get into a cat fight.

    Edwards seemed to have a drag account, he went to the comic bank for laughs so many times with this subject matter. Robert Vaughn tried out The Man from A.U.N.T. for size in Edwards' comedy S.O.B. Happily, this was only a small role.

 

Even William Powell put on a dress to get a laugh and co-star Myna Loy was on hand to show him the ropes in Love Crazy. Powell never resorted to drag in solving cases in The Thin Man series.

Another classic comic, Eddie Cantor tried passing himself off as just one of the girls in the bakery in Palmy Days. Cantor really fit right in with all those Goldwyn Girls.

 

 

Wearing too much make-up and slinky undergarments got William Hurt the Oscar for Kiss of a Spider WOman.

Robin Williams is simply inspirational as Mrs. Doubtfire. Robin dons his dull pinafors with hilarious aplumb, juggling breast prothestics and sagging pantyhose with acrobatic dexterity.

Some Like it Hot is vintage drag comedy. Much of the humor centers around musicians Joe and Jerry hiding out as Josephine and Daphne in an all girl's band.

Prison dramas have drag moments. The longest Yard has the funniest trio of cheerleaders ever to hike up their skirts for the sport.

Cary Grant is forced to pose as a woman in the often hilarious Howard Hawks flick I Was a Male War Bride.

    No, John Wayne never appeared in drag on screen, but Gerard Depardieu, the burly French star, did the makeover bit for Bertrand Blier's subversive comedy, Menage. Wayne would probably made up better.

In the forgettable Only Cowboys Get the Blues, John Hurt manages to show his ankles in drag as The Countess.

The Birdcage and the film it is based on, La Cage aux Follies are grand examples of making the most of make-up. Not only does Nathan Lane get to parade around showing off his "womanhood,' Gene Hackman gets in the act with a high heeled off balance disguise that adds another garment of laughs to the Mike Nichols directed film.
