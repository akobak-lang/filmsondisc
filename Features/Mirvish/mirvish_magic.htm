 Mirvish Magic

By Stu Kobak

If you saw him flitting in and out of film festival haunts you might think he was John Cusack in disguise, but Dan Mirvish's focus is on the other side of the camera. The boyish charm of the mid-thirtish filmmaker is like a little twister of energy fromt he Midwest. You can see it in his filmmaking, you can see it in his determination to help spread the message of independent film. You might even call him a whirling Mirvish.

     How did the eight year old Omaha get onto DVD.

"Basically, I got a call from a friend of mine, Scott Epstein, who works at Total Movie and Entertainment Magazine. He knew I had done Omaha but he had actually never seen it, but he was familiar with it. The magazine re-launched a few months ago and their sort of gimmick is that they put out a DVD with every issue. He said, Hey, how would you like to have Omaha on the flip side of our DVD. It has trailers and promotional things on one side and you can put a feature on the other. The funny thing is, he called me just before I was going to Park City for Slamdance this year. It was a really tight crunch for both of us. We scrambled around and a mutual friend had my master copy and we got it to him.

Dan met Scott through FilmFest DVD which Scott was a co-founder of and Dan served on their advisory board and was a consulting producer.

I guess I'd always been interested in movies growing up, but then in college got firmly into it. I was part of the student group that showed movies. It wasn't a film school, but they had a couple of courses. I took some classes at UCLA, some cinematography classes, and it was great. It was, wow, that what a real film school looks like. I produced some no smoking and coming soon trailers for my student film group.

After college I worked in Washington DC as a journalist. And then as a speech writer for Tom Harkins.

How do you get a job as a speech writer?

"I guess it was being in the right place at the right time because I wasn't really looking for it. I was working at The Washington Monthly Magazine, a small but prestigious political journal . It turned out that the head speech writer for Tom Harkins was a big fan of the Washington Monthly and wanted to write for them himself, so he thought he would get his in with the magazine by hiring me.

After I worked for Harkins for a couple of years, I decided to go to film school. Even while working for Harkin I did a fair amount of video production. There were a couple of video studios in the basement of the Capital that nobody knows about but all of us pay for. At USC a bunch of my friends were doing short films and people in Hollywood would say, well that's great kid but let us know when you've done a feature, which is very frustrating for a lot of people. This was bout the time Robert Rodriguez had done El Mariachi, and I figured if a guy from Texas for so little money, why not a guy from Omaha. And I found a little used thesis option as USC where you could do your Masters thesis, have it be a feature film and still own the rights, which at USC was a big deal because they normally own the rights to every film made there. Nobody had really done a feature there before.

Omaha was the first full length feature film done as a Masters thesis at USC. Tuition cost a lot less because you were doing a thesis project and one of the nice things about it was that you could apply all your student loans to the budget of the film.

When it came time to raise the bulk of the money for the film--I don't come from a particularly wealthy family and I don't have particularly wealthy friends--I don't have any exploitable ethnicities other than being another skinny Jewish white boy, but, from Omaha, and I thought that's the key. Make the film in Omaha. A friend of mine had told me about Carhenge and I knew a number of people who were actors in Omaha and I hadn't done any film stuff in Omaha, so I went back and talked to the Nebraska Film Commission and they were great. There had been very little independent filming in Nebraska so it was a pretty ripe place to find investors and get people excited about it. I knew I needed a local producer and I was told to hook up with this guy Dana, and it turned out that he was Robert Altman's grandson and that was a nice coincidence. So Robert helped us out a little bit. Dana and I called up every rich person in Omaha, including Warren Buffet (He wrote us a very nice letter saying that he was happy not to invest.) Eventually we cobbled the money together and it didn't come from too many rich people, but it came from friends and family and crew and cast family.

Using the mayor came from the film commissioner. I had written in a part for, quote, "the mayor" and I figured I'd just give it to an actor or an investor, but one of the first things the commission had me do was have a sit-down meeting with the mayor just to kind of announce that we were making the film to get the city on board to cooperate and during the meeting they said to the mayor, oh Dan's got a part int he film for the mayor--you should play it-- and he said, oh I'd love to--and that was it. During that same meeting he offered to put in $5000 of city money and $5000 of county money because of that. I thought to myself at the time, "Don't they need the money for schools and bridges and roads, and it turned out that I was right, they can't just give you money, but it did help us raise other money. The city did help us: they gave us free office space and if we needed a crane for a shot they just gave it to us. If we needed police there was no problem.

Mirvish got Omaha in the can and into the first few weeks of editing for $38000. The total including post production and distribution was $87,000.

The Wizard of Oz thread that manifests itself toward the end of Omaha and thereby comments on the whole film was never really intentional according to Mirvish. We shot most of the Carhenge scenes toward the beginning of the movie and Jill showed up in this outfit one day and it kind of dawned on all of us that there was this whole Dorothy thing going on there, so we explicitly decided at the time to put the no place like home line in the script and really did work. There really is a connection between The Wizard of Oz and my film, because if you remember at the end of the film when the wizard leaves in a balloon, he's going to Omaha. According to my high school history teacher is that when Frank Baum first wrote the story is was sort of an allegory for the gold standard and the yellow brick road was actually yellow bars of gold and the wizard was supposed to be William Jennings Bryan (Dominant Democratic turn of the century politician) who's from Nebraska. None of this was particularly conscious when I was writing the film.

After eight years since Omaha, Dan Mirvish has yet to get his next film in the can. There have been some false starts. 'The independent film world is very tough. There were times that there were being at least a thousand Indie films being made every year. At the time we made Omaha we premiered it at the Independent Feature Film Market in New York and we were the hot film there and a lot of distributors were talking to us. One of the distributors. And we didn't get into Sundance said if you get into Sundance we'll pick it up. At the time, if you got into Sundance, you weren't necessarily guaranteed distribution, but you wouldn't get distribution if you didn't get into Sundance. We did get a couple of smaller offers of distribution along the way but it was the kind of thing where they open the film in New York and LA and probably close it right away and if they do make money, you never see any of it. We always thought, meaning me and Dana, we should hold off on those kind of offers. We knew we could show the film in Nebraska. It would get shown. It was shot on 35mm. The irony is that today most people with the kind of money we had would shoot on DV, but you can't just walk into any theater with DV. Once we had a finished answer print we could walk into any theater in the world and just play Omaha. That's why we held off on the really smaller offers. Transferring a DVD to 35mm cost about forty thousand dollars.

And that led to the creation of Slamdance. We thought, all these buyers and press are in Park City--and we had heard of a couple individual people doings screenings the year before--so we thought why not get a whole group of filmmakers together. That was in '95. It was because of Omaha. It was all about us. We said, okay, what's the best strategy for us now that we did not get in to Sundance. It was because we didn't get into Sundance. And we had gotten into some other festivals, which which was probably why we didn't get into Sundance. We had already premiered at Mill Valley, we played at ISFM, we had gotten into Palm Springs, but for Sundance, they want premieres. We thought that going to Park City anyway was the best thing even if it meant starting our own festival and that's what we did. As it turned out that was a real critical turning point in the Independent film world since that was the year Sundance most noticeably starting taking films by second-time directors, established actors, bigger budgets and really stopped inviting these films right out of the ISFM which it had done in previous years. It was right about the time that Disney bought Miramax. It was a real changing time. At the same time there was a huge increase in the number of these low budget first time productions because people were influenced by the success of El Mariachi and later Clerks. That was the niche that ultimately Slamdance filled.

Stamp and Deliver, another Mirvish script, a modern day postal western, or as Mirvish puts it, it's a western without horses, a movie without computers, its a postal movie without Kevin Costner. We got these investors who were actually real estate people who dabbled in film, and they said if you come to Austin we'll make this film. And I knew a lot of people in Austin, we'd screened Omaha there. It's a real tight film community there. We went there and did almost two months of pre-production and we were five days from shooting and the financing fell through. It was very frustrating. We'd built huge sets and had actors down there rehearsing. Investors spent around two hundred thousand dollars. When the going got tough the tough got weird. At one point about a week before the shoot they wanted to cut twenty pages out of the script and it didn't matter which twenty pages-it wasn't a creative thing--they thought it was the best way to trim the budget. So they sent me and a friend of mine to a spa for the weekend--it was very strange--We think they were trying to do something odd with the back. So things eventually went south. The good news is that several years a couple of lawsuits later, the script is firmly back in my control. Last year Neil Young's company optioned the script. He really liked it and wanted to do the music for it. Peter Fonda wanted to be in it and Gary Busey, Dan Hedaya, Michael Lerner. Technically, the option has run out, but he's still committed to doing the music. So I'm hopeful that its going to get back on track.

In the meantime, there's another script, Open House, that I've written, and I'm getting ready to do that.

Basically you realize that the attention span of some of these guys is only about five minutes long.
