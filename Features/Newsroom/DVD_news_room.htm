Stars of the Laser News Room

By Stuart J. Kobak              

     I wonder what the first conceived image on film of a newspaperman looked like. He probably wearing was a rumpled suit, a cigarette dangling aggressively from his lips , capped by a worn fedora turned up rim at the front posing confrontational challenge. In a silent flick his pencil would move in quick broad strokes over his pad capturing the details of unfolding events. In an early talkies, he could have been wisecracking Pat O'Brien  in The Front Page or Lee Tracy in Doctor X,  a press card stuck in the ribbon round his fedora, speaking in the clipped and rapid fire staccato tones of a pressroom  typewriter frantically attacking a deadline.

                News people have been depicted in many of Hollywood's films, comfortably crossing genres from drama to action to suspense as well as comedy.  Hollywood's fascination with these players is not surprising. The reporters, journalists, editors, publishers, photographers, columnists,  all make for colorful film characterizations. News people have been ridiculed on film and depicted as champions of freedom or abusers of the public's sacred trust.

                Perhaps the most famous of those visions is Orson Welles' Citizen Kane. The story of Charles Foster Kane is not simply a tale of the newspaper business, but a tale of power and corruption, a fascinating example of screen craft at its greatest. Kane builds a newspaper empire from nothing,  Of course, for the most part, he does buy it. Without the unlimited funds from his family, it is questionable whether  Kane could have  pursued  his relentless quest to have the best and most influential cog in the public opinion making machine.

                Kane  had the money and ruthless drive necessary to bend his empire to his desired shape. His rise to power made me ponder the creation of my own ideal team of newsmen. Who are  the actors who best captured the spirit of these professionals on the screen?. Who would staff my ideal news room?

                I decided to limit my choices to those actors who had portrayed men of the news world on film. I further narrowed my choices with few exceptions, to selecting those performances that appear on laser.

                The top was as good a place as any to begin and why not Orson Welles. His performance as Kane is the definitive  mythic picture of the news mogul. Welles would play the role of publisher for  my  fictitious  empire. Others have done well capturing the role of publisher in movies. Edward Arnold in Frank Capra's  Meet John Doe creates convincing credentials, but the towering performance of Welles in Citizen Kane overpowers the less complex role  of publisher D. B. Norton created by Arnold. Vincent Price oozes with malevolence as a spoiled rich man playing with his newspaper as a toy in  the role of publisher in While the City Sleeps, a solid entry in the newspaper movie sweepstakes.

                With Welles installed as publisher, the challenge is pick the actor as editor who can stand up tothis powerful presence.  Spencer Tracy made several forays into the hyper world of daily journalism. In Libeled Lady, a delightful concoction of deviltry and dalliance, Tracy is wonderful in a comic take on  the role of big city editor. Tracy broadened his newspaper experience filling the shoes of a sports reporter in Women of the Year. This is the first pairing of Tracy with Katherine Hepburn and they are both splendid in this hilarious comedy directed by George Stevens. In the same year, again opposite Hepburn, Tracy played a reporter in the drama  Keeper of the Flame, However, it is not on the strength of these roles that I want Tracy as my editor. This actor has always embodied integrity, strength and honesty. When director  Stanley Kramer needed an actor that possessed those qualities for the role of Judge Dan Heywood in Judgment at Nuremberg, it was Tracy filling the shoes. Whether playing Clark Gable's conscience in San Francisco   Father Tim Mullen, or Shorty, Gable's rival in love and oil in Boom Town, Tracy measured up.      

                Welles and Tracy will butt heads and come close to ending their relationship time and again, but in the end they will together forge a greater publication. When Tracy wants talent, Welles will get it for him.

                Donald Woods is a real life man who risked everything for the sake of truth. In Cry Freedom, Kevin Kline takes on the role of Woods, a South African newspaper editor, and paints a typically solid Kline portrait. Although Kline's newspaper resume is limited to this one role, he is capable of being the man I want on my team when the chips are down. The role in this fine Richard Attenborough film is enough to secure him the job as managing editor under Tracy's guiding hand.

                The editorial management team can use some explosiveness, some competitive fire, some pugnacious combativeness to keep the collective juices flowing. James Cagney can comfortably slip from gangster garb, role up his sleeves and fit this role to a tee. Cagney has the screen experience too. In Johnny Come Lately he plays Tom Richards, a disillusioned newspaper man on the bum. When he is picked up for vagrancy in a small town he is passing through, he is seduced into using his talent as a newsman to  lead a crusade against the corrupt powers of the town. This was Cagney's initial production as an independent and perhaps the choice of material says something of the image Cagney sought for himself. It's seems reasonable that Cagney would be delighted by the role provided for him on this dream newspaper staff. His follow-up film, Blood on the Sun, is set in Japan in the late 1920's. Once again Cagney plays a newsman, this time the tough guy editor of the Tokyo Chronicle who helps expose a plot outlining Japan's quest for world domination. Cagney's last experience as a newspaperman is in Come Fill the Cup, but the focus of this drama is alcoholism not journalism.

                That pretty much completes the management team. I can see Welles constantly sparking conflict with this trio. Tracy and Cagney are bound to go head to head; however, Kline is on hand to temper potential explosions.

                The most experienced  journalistic actor turns out to be Clark Gable. Gable has paid his dues from reporter to war correspondent to big city editor, and even with  some time behind the newsreel cameras in Too Hot Too Handle. When Clark Gable appeared alongside Claudette Colbert in Frank Capra's award winning It Happened One Night, he defined the image of the wise cracking, world-wise reporter. Gable displayed his unique combination of charm and street smarts, in the process beguiling Claudette Colbert and forever capturing the devotion of the movie-going public.  Not only did Gable surprise the industry with his talent for comedy, he won his only Academy Award for Best Actor in this role. It Happened One Night is mad-cap comedy at its finest and along with Gable's Oscar, the film swept the awards for Best Actress, Best Director, and Best Picture. Gable's last outing in journalistic get-up was in Teacher's Pet, another wonderful comedy turn, this time opposite a battling Doris Day. During the several decades in between he filled various news roles in the films  Love on the Run, Somewhere I'll find You and Comrade X.

                Whereas playing footsie with Doris Day is a wonderful role for Gable, in newsman terms it is not his defining persona. No, Gable belongs on the streets, making his way into forbidden places using all the weaponry of his macho image and sex appeal.        Gable will work perfectly on this dream newspaper. He's smart, tough and savvy. He's the crack reporter by strength of his audacity combined with an affability molded into a golden key able to unlock any door. Gable will get the story. Naturally, Gable will get all the plum assignments from Tracy. He owes him that.

                As a complement to the raw reportorial bravura of Gable, Gregory Peck is an ideal choice to provide a balance of polish and sophistication. Peck's primary experience in the field was  opposite Audrey Hepburn in  William Wyler's stylishly charming Roman Holiday.  Although Peck did nothing to earn him a place on the dream staff  in this lighthearted romp through Rome, nor in his additional role in newspaper robes as a sportswriter in Designing Woman, these roles at least provide the excuse to place him on staff. Peck throughout his career has conveyed an aura of intelligence on the screen. In roles like Atticus Finch in To Kill a Mockingbird  he displayed a range of human understanding that make him an interesting choice to specialize as our political reporter.

                This news staff is quickly turning into a male fraternal order. Have no fear and hold your sides because Rosalind Russell is the perfect cure for taking this venture too seriously. Russell is breathtakingly hyper and startlingly stunning as reporter Hildy Johnson in Howard Hawk's His Girl Friday, the  smashing remake of The Front Page. Russell stakes claim to the madcap crown of frenetic journalism. Russell's energy will keep the news room fast and loose and despite getting her job on the strength of this comic tour-de-force, she'll prove she can go out and get the story with the best of them.

                Along for the ride on this roller coaster of hilarity is Cary Grant, matching Russell's breathless banter barb for wit. His Girl Friday was Grant's one newspaper role and he acquits himself wonderfully as Hildy's editor, Walter Burns; nevertheless, I will pass on the great temptation of finding a place for Grant on the staff.

                 I can think of no actress that captured the hards-as-nails woman on screen like Barbara Stanwyck.  The ice in Stanwyck's veins was frozen over twice. She had icicles protruding from her fingertips. In roles such as Phyllis Dietrichson in Billy Wilder's fabulous Double Indemnity, she was more than a match for her male counterparts. Stanwyck stood up to the big boys in Meet John Doe as Ann Mitchell, the news writer who's inspiration creates "John Doe" and falls in love with his incarnation, Gary Cooper's home spun Long John Willoughby.   And Stanwyck's experience behind the typewriter is not limited to just one film. In To Please a Lady, as a newspaper columnist she matched wits against Clark Gable taking him to task before finally falling to his masculine charms. There is no question that the boys on this news team will admire Stanwyck's tough sophistication while ogling her gams. Stanwyck's inclusion as a reporter on this laser news team will strengthen  and add some beauty to the balance.

                 Jean Arthur is another stand-out actress who  surely fits the right no-nonsense image for this news room. Her performance as big city reporter Babe Bennett in Frank Capra's Mr Deeds Goes to Town, is not available on disc, but failing to include Arthur  and her signature sardonic screen wit would be cheating our news staff of the delight of her presence and the inspiration of her biting cynicism. So Arthur joins Russell and Stanwyck giving our newspaper the best distaff trio of journalists desire can buy.

                At the sports desk it's Humphrey Bogart. One of Bogart's greatest roles is that of sportswriter turned publicist in The Harder They Fall. Directed by Mark Robson from an original screenplay by Budd Schulberg, the film is a masterful, power punching indictment of the prize fight "racket." Bogart's Eddie Willis etches the image of the cynical sportswriter into the subconscious of the movie going public for all time. This was Humphrey Bogart's final film before succumbing to cancer.

                With calculated malice magnified behind  severe spectacles,  Burt Lancaster wields the power of a Broadway gossip columnist with nauseatingly cold glee  in The Sweet Smell of Success, the reference role of its type. Lancaster is a hands down choice to rule the roost surrounded by the baying horde of small time publicists vying for minute plugs of space within his daily column. Clearly, Lancaster will isolate himself from the balance of this dream staff. The image of J. J. Hunsecker dangling snippets of hope before an oily Tony Curtis playing on his knees opposite him in "Sweet Smell of Success."  should make his co-workers wary of any relationship.

                The drama desk is a toss-up between George Sanders, whose credentials feature the role of Addison Dewitt in the seminal bitchy theater film All About Eve, and Clifton Webb whose Waldo Lydecker in Laura has become the textbook measure of acerbic screen sophistication. My choice was made easy by the fact that I also need a movie critic(not being available for the job myself owing to prior obligations). Sanders won the Oscar for Best Supporting Oscar in his role as a theater critic so I decided to bestow the stodgy seat on him. On the other hand, the role of film critic provides a marvelous forum for the acid wit and erudition of Clifton Webb. Webb is more likely to use the dagger system as his symbol for  rating films rather than the somewhat lusterless and  ubiquitous star.

                Joe Pesci did an excellent  100 minutes as the classic news photographer in the rather underrated film The Public Eye last year. As forties flashbulb changer Bernzy Bernstein, Pesci mixed the right  amount of down and dirty determination with would-be romantic hope to capture the seat as staff photographer. Pesci proved that he's willing to go to any lengths to get the picture. Too bad more people didn't see this picture, which had lots of style but suffered from some hackneyed mystery scripting.

                Our overseas bureau has to be staffed by a number of foreign correspondents and why not start with Joel McCrea. The lanky and likable McCrea starred in the 1940 Alfred Hitchcock spy thriller Foreign Correspondent as the title character. McCrea more than earns his place on our staff in this role.  With rather more serious credentials, Sam Waterston joins the ranks of our overseas crew with his sterling portrayal of New York Times correspondent Sidney Schanberg in The Killing Fields. Waterston exhibited a cold efficiency in that film that may not have earned him a place in the hearts of filmgoers, but deservedly returns him to his role as foreign correspondent for our dream publication.  A delightful edition to the staff is Gene Hackman by virtue of his role as Alex Grazier in the 1983 film Under Fire. Hackman is seldom less than great in any film and his reputation holds up in Under Fire. One last player, reluctantly added to the overseas contingent is James Woods. In Salvador, directed by Oliver Stone,  Woods plays sleazy correspondent Richard Boyle to such perfection that it still makes my flesh crawl to write about him.  Nevertheless, we need someone we can count as expendable and Woods is the guy that gets the assignment a correspondent is not likely to come back from. He'll probably come back though; and with a measure of grudging respect for his ability to get the story and survive in the process, he'll keep getting fed those death defying jobs.

                In The Man Within, Jurgen Prochnow plays real life German newspaperman Gunter Wallraff. The film is an indictment of unscrupulous journalistic practices,  as Wallraff works incognito on the staff of the German daily "The Standard" to expose its corruption. Prochnow deserves our investigative/undercover seat. No one is likely to recognize this fine actor.

                I had to find a place for Albert Brooks. He qualifies for his role as a television journalist and would-be, unfortunately sweaty, prime time news anchor in the highly successful James Brooks directed comedy Broadcast News.  Brooks all but steals the film. We haven't yet expanded into the television media with our dream news staff, but Brooks will certainly be comfortable doing a daily column  from his own hilarious perspective. Count on him to drive everyone crazy in our news room.  Tracy will be pulling his hair out, but turning his back with a hidden smile on his face.

                We had to cut costs at some point and our budget simply would not allow for the hiring of additional talent at this point(I thought Welles would buy Tracy anything he wanted). I suppose we should have included James Stewart on our staff for his role as Macauley Conner in The Philadelphia Story, or Gene Kelly as E. K. Hornbeck, the reporter covering the Cates trial in Inherit the Wind. Jack Lemmon played a farcical Hildy Johnson in the Billy Wilder remake of The Front Page, but that role was clearly not up to Rosalind Russell's previous gender bending achievement in His Girl Friday.               

                 Kurt Russell didn't make the staff even though he turned in a  credible performance as a reporter in The Mean Season.   After a lengthy and heated discussion, management decided that Russell lacked the experience to join the staff.  John Ireland, an otherwise reliable "B" performer, had a splendid role in All the King's Men, but we decided to disqualify him on the basis that he was more a witness than a reporter and that film belongs to Broderick Crawford in the Huey Long type role. Dustin Hoffman's Carl Bernstein in All the President's Men is absent owing to its unavailability on laser; the same goes for Jason Robards in that film. We considered using Sally Field for her role in the Sidney Pollack directed Absence of Malice, but we were afraid she might screw up some story  with catastrophically   misdirected diligence. Not only that, but she might have brought the unions down on our management, as suggested by her wonderful union maid in Norma Rae. Perhaps there's no excuse for ignoring Jon Voight in The Odessa File, but the fact of the matter is we'd rather see him in Midnight Cowboy, Runaway Train or Deliverance. Kirk Douglas would surely have secured a place on the staff for his role in Billy Wilder's Ace in the Hole had that film been available on laser disc. Douglas gives a marvelous performance as Wilder directs at his cynically best. Peter Breck stars as a journalist investigating a mental institution in Sam Fuller's Shock Corridor. Peter who?  Warren Beatty is journalist John Reed in Reds, but he's more revolutionary than reporter, so it wasn't hard to pass him by. And then there's Pat O'Brien in the original The Front Page, but that's disqualified for absence of laser, while Lee Tracy in Doctor X has a voice that would drive everyone else out of the city room.             

                So there you have it. We put together what we believe to be a  credible staff. Now all we have to do is begin getting out our daily; and who knows how long that will survive the pressures of the market place. Welles claims we're losing money already and there is a nasty, but persistent, and not unlikely rumor that Mr. Welles is getting tired of this paper toy and is considering joining the bidding war for Paramount. Can you imagine? Welles owning a studio.  Hmmmm.

The Discs

Citizen Kane A, A

US/1941/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/119 minutes/Directed by Orson Welles/Starring Orson Welles, Joseph Cotton/Voyager/44 Chaps/Supplements/3 discs/CAV/$124.95

                Welles' seminal debut directoral effort is a wondrously satisfying trip into the world of Charles Foster Kane. This special edition is for the Kane-a-maniacs. It is a first rate transfer and pressing. Details are lovingly depicted and there is a wealth of supplemental material included.

Meet John Doe B+, C

US/1941/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/123 minutes/Directed by Frank Capra/Starring Gary Cooper, Barbara Stanwyck, Edward Arnold/Laservision Associates/20 Chaps/2 discs/CLV-CAV/$29.95

                Though somewhat dated, this Capra directed sermonizer manages to still be moving and entertaining. Gary Cooper's fine, as are Stanwyck and Arnold. It's a title worth owning.

                My copy of the disc exhibited undue anounts of pressing related noise and made this an unacceptable disc. Otherwise, the films elements used for the transfer were fine and the disc would have been a worthwhile purchase.

While the City Sleeps, B,B+

US/1957/B&W/Widescreen/Mono/100 minutes/Directed by Fritz Lang/Starring Dana Andrews, Ida Lupino/Image Ent/CLV/$19.98

                This atmospheric suspensor from noir icon Fritz Lang is more entertaining for its choice of characters than the  mystery that unfolds around them. Set among the shadows of the city streets and the banter of the big city news room, the film is a good evening's entertainment.

                Some of the stand out acting turns include Ida Lupino and George Sanders, back biting at each other, with Vincent Price  gleefully overseeing the in-fighting.

                The images have been transferred crisply and Lang's wonderful sense of style has not been diminished one iota. The sound is all right, but the range of black to white stands out.

Woman of the Year B+, B

US/1942/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/114 minutes/Directed by George Stevens/Starring Spencer Tracy, Katherine Hepburn/MGM-UA/CLV/$34.98

                Delightful and often hilarious battle of the sexes directed by master George Stevens. Tracy and Hepburn are wonderful in their first screen sparring match, with Tracy as sportswriter and Hepburn sophisticated international columnist. The kitchen scene is unforgettable.

                This laser disc was pressed before chapter stops were the accepted practice. The transfer is very handsome with beautiful deep blacks and an excellent contrast ratio, except for a few dark scenes which were washed out and lacking in detail. Certainly a collector's must.

Cry Freedom, B+, B

US/1987/Color/Pan & Scan 1.33:1/Stereo Surround/157 minutes/Directed by Richard Attenborough/Starring Kevin Kline, Denzel Washington/MCA-Universal/No Chaps/2 discs/CLV/$39.98

                Attenborough's fine take on South African apartheid is at times a devastating experience. Based on the true story of editor Donald Woods and black activist Stephen Biko, this is a thoroughly involving film.

                Kevin Kline and Denzel Washington as Woods and Biko are in top form.

                The disc has been produced pan and scan unfortunately. Had it been released today it would have been presented widescreen. It still looks very good and while compositions suffer at times, the transfer is clean and pleasing. The sound is very good.

Johnny Come Lately, B, B

US/1943/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/99 minutes/Directed by

William K. Howard/Starring James Cagney, Grace George/Republic Pictures Home Video/No Chaps/CLV/$34.98

                Enjoyable but thin drama with Cagney taking up the crusade against corruption in a small town. The performances by Cagney and Grace George are appealing. It was filmed in a straight forward  style to good effect.

                The disc image is reasonably sharp. Black and white levels look good. There are some speckles. The sound is passable.

It Happened One Night A-, F

US/1934/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/105 minutes/Directed by Frank Capra/Starring Clark Gable, Claudette Colbert/Columbia-Tristar/No Chaps/CLV/$24.95

                One of the original madcap comedies that sets the standard for "road" movies for years to come. This was Gable's breakthrough role which brought his star to the fore. He and Colbert are wonderful together and Capra liberally uses his magic wand to conduct this symphony of comic minuets.

                The disc is totally unacceptable. Most of the transfer is marred by a maddeningly soft  image. Even if these were the best available elements for transfer, the poor quality ruins the pleasure of the film. This is certainly a title worthy of better treatment. Perhaps the expense of a restoration is in order. Until then you have to pass on this title.

Teacher's Pet A-, B+

US/1957/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/120 minutes/Directed by George Seaton/Starring Clark Gable, Doris Day, Gig Young/Paramount/No Chaps/2 discs/CLV/$39.95

                This is a comfortably delightful vehicle for the talents of Gable and Day. Gable's cool and Day's ebullience mix a cocktail full of smiles in this comedy set around the rules of journalism. Gig Young slurs through his practiced and perfected picture of inebriation providing  moments of mirth.

                The disc looks excellent. Detail is very sharp. Blacks are beautiful. There is no excess of grain. City room details have been lovingly transferred and the soundtrack is enjoyable.

Roman Holiday B+, B

US/1953/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/118 minutes/Directed by William Wyler/Starring Audrey Hepburn, Gregory Peck/Paramount/No Chaps/CLV/$34.95

                Audrey Hepburn is so enchanting as a princess on the lam that this is really her picture. She and Peck work well together. The comedy is not likely to have you holding your sides for pain of laughter, however you are guaranteed a running smile for the almost two hours running time.

                The transfer has been made from good elements. The images are sharp and there are only minor speckles and scratches. I noted some scenes with a fairly soft contrast ratio, but overall this is a good and pleasing disc.

                Designing Woman, C+, B

US/1957/Color/Widescreen 2.25:1/Mono/118 minutes/Directed by Vincente Minnelli/Starring Gregory Peck, Lauren Bacall/MGM-UA/53 Chaps/CLV/$34.98

                This minor Minnelli directed comedy  has production values and stars that add up to a total less than the ingredients. Peck and Bacall are all right together, but they certainly to not provide the spark that other more successful romantic screen pairings have provided their films. Mickey Shaughnessy is very funny is a supporting role as a punchy prize fighter.

                There is excessive grain in the transfer and the colors, which appear slightly wan, tend toward orange. The mono sound is very good.

Sweet Smell of Success A, B

US/1957/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/97 minutes/Directed by Alexander Mackendrick/Starring Burt Lancaster, Tony Curtis/MGM-UA/No Chaps/CLV/$34.98

                One of the truly great depictions of the Broadway rat race. Burt Lancaster is magnificent as columnist J.J. Hunsecker and Tony Curtis plays perhaps his best screen role as Sidney Falco, the desperate publicist. The film is blessed by the beautiful photography of James Wong Howe. The streets of Broadway have never looked better. The Elmer Bernstein score likewise captures the essence of the script and scene.

                A good looking transfer captures the garish contrast of Howe's image of the Times Square night. There is a minor excess of grain in the pressing, but the transfer images are sharp. The sound is fine. Overall, this is a collector's best bet.

All About Eve A, C+

US/1950/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Stereo/138 minutes/Directed by Joseph L. Mankiewicz/Starring Bette Davis, Ann Baxter, George Sanders/Fox Video/No Chaps/2 discs/CLV/$49.98

                The witty and articulate tail of Broadway bitchiness remains a classic entertainment. Bette Davis is a gem as Margo Channing and George Sanders is erudition itself as Addison DeWitt.  Definitely a pleasure to watch.

                The disc exhibited classic evidence of the beginning of laser rot. The image was all right, although the contrasts were somewhat soft. The rot problem, naturally, makes the disc a no-no.

Laura A, B

US/1944/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/88 minutes/Directed by Otto Preminger/Starring Gene Tierney, Dana Andrews, Clifton Webb/Fox Video/13 Chaps/CLV/$39.98

                Ranks with the best of the noir classics. Laura has an elegant shape to it. It moves in graceful, economic steps to its satisfying end. Its style is defined from the opening moments when Detective Mark McPherson interviews Waldo Lydecker as the writer lounges decadently in his bathtub with a typewriter propped before him. 

                The players are just fine. Tierney is beautiful, Andrews sincere, and Webb, pretty much stealing the show, is nothing short of superb.

                For the most part this is a fairly good looking disc, although there are some unaccountably soft  focus scenes. There are a usual number of speckles and minor scratches for material of this age. The soundtrack exhibited a barely detectable background hum.

Public Eye, The B, B+

US/1992/Color/Widescreen 1.66:1/Stereo Surround/99 minutes/Directed by Howard Franklin/Starring Joe Pesci, Barbara Hershey/MCA-Universal/27 Chaps/CLV/$34.98

                This is a very enjoyable film that along with a fine performance from Pesci benefits from a plush production treatment. The photography, although very dark, has a very handsome look, and the insertion of stop frame black and white stills isvery interesting. Hersey fits into the 1930's period just as well as she fills a clinging dress.  The  ultimate success of the film is held back by a rather pedestrian and confusing script.

                The disc has been given a first rate transfer, however, those dark images are difficult on video. Nevertheless, it is accurate and very watchable. The sound track is richly presented. A good bet for an enjoyable time.

Foreign Correspondent A-, B+

US/1940/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/120 minutes/Directed by Alfred Hitchcock/Starring Joel McCrea, Loraine Day/Warner Home Video/37 Chaps/2 Discs/CLV-CAV/$39.98

                Foreign Correspondent is crackerjack entertainment from master director Alfred Hitchcock. It has been beautifully filmed and tightly scripted. Joel McCrea has been perfectly cast in the role of the American correspondent who becomes embroiled in a kidnapping plot by Nazi agents. The quixotic allusions of the windmill sequence are a special treat.

                The transfer is quite lovely to look at. The full range of the gray scale has been ably captured. There is minor speckling throughout the disc set and the mono sound has been handled very well. A welcome edition to your disc collection.

Man Inside,  The B, B

US/1990/Color/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/93 minutes/Directed by Bobby Roth/Starring Jurgen Prochnow, Peter Coyote/New Line Home Video/No Chaps/CLV/$34.99

                This is an exciting and well directed effort  dealing with the  real life story of a German undercover journalist. Jurgen Prochnow disappears well into the role. The overall look of the production is not up to the excellent subject material. The photography is not outstanding. The film does make for enjoyable viewing and the tale is sympathetic.

                The transfer is sharp. While some tones lean to orange, the colors are otherwise strong and true. The sound is a bit thin but always clear.

Broadcast News B+, C+

US/1987/Color/Pan and Scan 1.33:1/Stereo/132 minutes/Directed by James L. Brooks/Starring William Hurt, Holly Hunter, Albert Brooks/Fox Video/No Chaps/2 discs/CLV/$49.98

                Brooks' often hilarious look at the broadcasting business is a very enjoyable film. Albert Brooks, in a major supporting role turns in a sparkling performance. William Hurt and Holly Hunter are equally good. The are a number of fabulous set pieces that make this film a particular success. Brooks' debut as the local news anchor man will bring tears of laughter to your eyes, and Joan Cusack's charge through the broadcast offices to deliver an edited tape is breathless hilarity.

                Unfortunately, my disc is made unwatchable by severe laser rot. Otherwise, the transfer is reasonable, although some scenes do suffer from cropping. This is not the first faulty disc

 set of Broadcast News I've seen.  Perhaps there is a repressing that may be fine. Buyer beware!

Philadelphia Story, The A, B

US/1940/B&W/Fullscreen 1.33:1/Mono/112 minutes/Directed by George Cukor/Starring Cary Grant, Katherine Hepburn, James Stewart/MGM-UA/49 Chaps/Theatrical Trailer/CLV/$34.98

                George Cukor has directed this story of the sabotaging of a high society marriage with extreme grace and style. Cukor has at his command three stars at their peaks of screen power, and the resultant chemistry couldn't be better. The lovely humor draws itself not only from the plot line but the delicious depiction of the characters. This is a can't miss evening's entertainment.

                The transfer is marred by a mild softness in quite a few scenes, but it does not disrupt from the pleasure of the finely preserved black and white film stock. There are minor speckles and scratches in the transfer print, but it is generally clean and the soundtrack is fine.

Front Page, The B, C+

US/1974/Color/Pan & Scan 1.33:1/Mono/105 minutes/Directed by Billy Wilder/Starring Jack Lemmon, Walter Matthau/MCA-Universal/18 Chaps/Theatrical Trailer/CLV/$34.98

                While still quite funny, this is probably the weakest of three treatments of this story. It is surprising that Wilder was not more successful in adapting this, but I think it suffers from the casting of Jack Lemmon and Walter Matthau in the roles of Hildy Johnson and Walter Burns.

                The film was shot anamorphic Panavision and composition suffers greatly in transfer to !.33 aspect ratio. The color is stable and reasonably rich and the image is sharp. This is not up to the usual high MCA standard, however.

Announced for laser from Voyager, but delayed because they have not yet found film elements that they are totally happy with.
