Politics As Usual

By Stu Kobak

      Every year there's a new look at politics or political figures on film. Each generation of filmmakers takes a stab at understanding the past and the present through film. A political movie can send out its message from the biographical point of view. Some biographies do nothing more than skirt the issue of politics and touch episodically on the lives of our heroes and leaders.  There are films, like JFK from Oliver Stone, that examine one event in the life of a political figure, in this case the assassination of president Kennedy. It's a politicized  film with an acknowledged biased point of view. Stone makes a case for the conspiracy theory, ponders different possibilities and simply won't go away when confronted by the stone wall of government. Here's a filmmaker with the power of his convictions.
     Most recently in Nixon, Stone takes on an entirely different look at history and politics. He tries to analyze what made Nixon who he was, what made him act, what drove him and prevented him from attaining the greatness and acceptance he wanted more than anything else.
     One of my favorite political flicks, a timeless essay on corruption and the harsh realities of politics, is not a biography or recreation of historical events. Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, made in 1939, stands the test of time, often providing a fresh insight, a renewal of hope, or the pain of the reality of our political system.
     Recently The American President made a pass at politics in the pure white city, this time in the guise of a light romantic comedy. It's another view from Hollywood.          
      Dave, a modern comic take on the oval office, is an amiable satire from director Ivan Reitman . International politics, an integral part of Nixon   as he opens up China or evaluates the political considerations of bombing Hanoi or Cambodia, is given the broadside in an English comedy The Mouse That Roared. The political satire is fun. Even the great Dr. Strangelove takes its shots at international politics as President Merkin J.Muffey pleadingly negotiates with his Soviet counterpart, Dimitiri. Some of the best political satire ever is distilled in Kubrick's war room.
     I come to bury Nixon not to praise him. Just kidding. Nixon  is a very fine movie. It's not perfect and its chief failure, albeit one you can't get away from, is the casting of Anthony Hopkins as Richard Nixon. There is no physical similarity between the two men. The scanty attempts at making Hopkins look more like Nixon are ludicrous at best.  When you can't cut the mustard, eat the hot dog plain.
      Director Oliver Stone's attempt to make Nixon a tragic and sympathetic figure fails hands down because of the Hopkins syndrome. It is interesting to realize that Nixon is most successful when Hopkins is not the center of the frame and that the events playing out are not dependent on believing that the actor is really Nixon.
            It's not totally fair to fault Hopkins. It was Stone that threw him into the pit of performing  snakes. All the actors around him are so fantastic. It takes James Woods little more than a buzz-cut hair style to create Bob Haldeman to perfection.. Paul Sorvino is uncanny as Henry Kissinger, covered by successful make-up and sporting a marvelous German accent.  It's almost as if it is an effort for Joan Lewis to stand up as she staggers convincingly through the role of Pat Nixon. She makes her story almost more interesting than Nixon's. Here is a woman who has been abused and shut out by the devious tunnel vision of her husband. Yet it seems she too is seduced by the power and the glory beyond her loyalty to her husband. She's much prettier than Pat Nixon and really doesn’t look like her, yet she in no way disturbs the illusion.  Bob Hoskins oozes knowing malice tinged by perversion as J. Edgar Hoover.
      Hopkins, sadly, is an actor cavorting in a monkey suit,  every once in a while dropping his jaw to set his teeth in a pathetic smile trying to replicate the Nixon look. The barrier is insurmountable even in the actor's best moments, when he dances with the devil across the floor of ambition, gracefully doing the two-step around decency and morality without ever looking at his feet.
       Stone is a daring story teller unafraid of his medium. The director moves back and forth in time, uses special effects to make a point of passing time, switches to black and white, utilizing rapid fire editing and everything else at his command. Consequently, Nixon is a fascinating study in filmmaking. It is never traditional in its approach and weaves in and out of Nixon's life in a free association blaze of directing skill.
       Nixon is a ferociously entertaining laser disc, an unswerving personal vision of a skilled and daring director. Make no mistake about, this is Stone's view of history, which he makes clear from the very outset of the film. It's a beauty of a laser disc with sharp, distinct images, whether in black and white or color. The 2.35:1 aspect ratio is perfectly framed. Included in the handsome pull-out box package is an entire side of deleted or expanded scenes introduced by the comments of director Stone. Listening to Stone makes his vision of the life and times of Richard Nixon perfectly clear making this special edition of Nixon even more powerful and illuminating.
       You cannot watch Nixon and hear his mangled  reference to Bernstein and Woodward without wanting to hurry into the laser library to pull out All the Presidents Men. Here's filmmaking in a totally different style. It's fascinating to watch the Watergate cover-up from the inside and then watch the Watergate "un-coverup" from the outside. While Nixon suffers from a great lead actor inn the wrong role, All the President's Men is issued instant press credentials with the casting of Robert Redford and Dustin Hoffman as Washington Post reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein.
     Alan J. Pakula directs this  tale of  two hungry, determined  reporters on the scent of political stench, turning it into an excellent thriller. When you know the outcome of a film, the great challenge is maintaining tension. All the President's Men achieves this beautifully. Even the precipitous ending works in its favor since the director instinctively understands that to play out the entire scenario of Watergate will only slowly drain the pulsing blood of his story.
     This view of Watergate is also blessed with excellent supporting players. Jason Robards gets an Academy Award nod as Benjamin Bradlee, Editor of the Washington Post.  Robards commands the screen with enough strength to stand up to the powerful screen personas of Redford and Hoffman.
            Warner presents an outstanding laser disc of All the President's Men. In the shadows or the florescent lit press rooms, detail is impeccable. Detailed with 35 chapters, the contrast and brilliance of this laser is consistently maintained. The sound is strong, thought the surround information is not aggressive.
            The Mouse that Roared is a broad political spoof about walnut sized country whose only export and source of income is threatened by an American imitation. An inspired Prime Minister played by the daffy Peter Sellers determines that the only way to survive is declare war on the United States. After all, he reasons,  the nations defeated by the United States all come out with the lion's share of financial support after the war.
            This inspired course of action is sanctioned by parliament and supported by the Grand Duchy, a dowager also played by Peter Sellers.  The hereditary field marshal and game warden Tully Bascombe is called upon to lead the expedition against the US. Naturally, he is also played by Peter Sellers.            Bascombe leads his crossbow armed warriors to America aboard a passenger ship.  As the troops prepare on deck for their invasion and quick defeat, Bascombe spends his time bent over the ship’s rail retching with each sway.  
     Fenwick’s invasion force lands during an air raid drill in New York City . Tully's initiative foils the best laid plans of Prime Ministers and he returns to Grand Fenwick in possession of the new and devastating Q bomb, its inventor and his daughter. No, Peter Sellers does not play the scientist nor his daughter, thank God. Political upheaval follows as the great powers line up at the borders of Grand Fenwick in an attempt to form an alliance between themselves and Fenwick, holder of the Q bomb.       

            The Mouse That Roared is a  soft,  good-natured satire, a huge British tongue poking into the global cheek. The laser disc is a fair looking product transferred from mediocre elements. Color is slightly washed out but it doesn’t make the film less colorful. The 1959 films is a brisk 83 minutes. The mono sound provides clean dialogue.

            The defining image for me in all these Hollywood political takes is the pure and innocent Jefferson Smith, disappointed, distraught, disillusioned and desperate, sitting before the Lincoln monument pondering the dirt that has forever sullied his purity. In juxtaposition, and perhaps intentionally, Stone brings Nixon to the same place to look for comfort from the monument to Honest Abe. Smith, touched by the despicable reality of politics and Nixon, the dirty trickster and betrayer of the presidency, each worshipping at the alter of presidential legend. It makes one ponder the motivations that drive people to their particular space of land.
            The granddaddy of political corruption movies is Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, one of my all time favorite films. No matter how many times I see this film it never fails to bring tears to my eyes or choke me up at various moments. The exact scene is  not quite predictable and I can’t be sure if it’s “sense memory” bringing me back to an earlier time when the cynical strains of adulthood hadn’t yet taken over my being.  “Smith” is a fresh experience for me each time. I have never marveled quite so at the brilliance of James Stewart as the wide-eyed Senator. I just sort of took his passion for granted. It is a brilliant performance. This is a great film and must rank amongst my very favorites.
            Mr. Smith Goes to Washington was a very controversial film in its day. Filmed with war raging in Europe, many were sensitive to director Capra’s  portrayal of corruption in Washington’s highest places. Good triumphs over evil at the conclusion of the film, but the queasiness over the  extent of political corruption is forever an unsettling reminder of what goes on behind closed doors in the power centers of our country.
            Director Frank Capra got it just right with Mr. Smith Goes to Washington.  Capra combines charm, humor, drama, and Americana brilliantly. No element of this film is out of harmony. Sentimental scenes of Jeff Smith contemplating the world in the shadow of the Lincoln Memorial are balanced by a delicious bit of business with a hat as Smith meets socialite Susan Paine.  Capra’s actors are at their best in Mr. Smith Goes to Washington. Alongside Stewart’s great work, Jean Arthur brings her cynical screen siren to delightful life. Claude Raines’ eloquent delivery of dialogue as Senator Joseph Paine resonates with the haunting beauty of loss. Edward Arnold is equally brilliant as bullying political boss Jim Taylor, Thomas Mitchell compliments Arthur’s work wonderfully as correspondent Diz Moore, and Harry Carey brings a generous helping of humanity to the role as Vice President.
            Overall, this laser reissue of the 1939 Mr. Smith Goes to Washington is a very good looking disc. Some evidence of film element damage is visible, but it is mostly confined to specs of dirt or an errant scratch, and  it never gets in the way of the pure pleasure provided by this beautiful film. The black and white palette is presented with a full range and the film has been transferred in consistently crisp visual style. The mono sound does not have a harsh edge nor is there an undue amount of recording hiss.  There are 69 chapter, a theatrical trailer and two discs in this CLV set priced at $44.95 from Columbia Tri-star Home Video. This screen classic belongs in every laser collection. It is truly a part of our heritage.
            Mr.  Smith Goes to Washington was the film that first instilled a healthy measure of cynicism in my developing mind. Capra was a brave filmmaker laying down his challenge to America just as current filmmakers like Oliver Stone are unafraid to lay bare America to celluloid scrutiny. Each new political film can serve as a sharp reminder of the freedom we enjoy in America and the need to preserve it,
