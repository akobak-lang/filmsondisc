Event Horizon/C,A-

Paramount/1997/105m/WS 2.35

    Derivitive and unoriginal, Event Horizon may have a polished production, but it's narrative falls precipitiously into a black hole. There are elements of the wonderful Robert Wise film The Haunting, but they are not effective in this high tech outer space setting. The production plays more like an extended Star Trek episode, without the charm of the very familar crew.
     Investigating the sudden reappearance of the spaceship Event Horizon which diappeared on an space expedition almost a decade ago, a space led by Captain Miller and Dr. William Weir, the scientist who designed the Event Horizon, find the ship armed with a power they could only have imagined. Event Horizon, or some alien power aboard her, takes control of the investigating team one by one, turning them against each other with predictable results.

A new face for Sam Neill©Paramount

 

 

 

 

 

     The usually very reserved Sam Neill trys his hand at overacting and it doesn't suit him too well. When Dr. Weir is caught up in the power of Event Horizon, Neill is caught up in a bad script. Lawrence Fishbourne tries to act tough as Captain Miller, but it's really a tough trip to bring to life.
     This very good looking film has been transferred into a handsome laser disc. Colors are intense and rich, shadow detail very good. The sharp images are sometimes too much for the set design's straight edges, leaving them a jumble of NTSC artifacts. Look for a terriically agressive Dolby Digital 5:1 mix. The explosions are visual and sonic extravanganzas.

Thieves Like Us/B+,B

 

Columbia/1996/107m

    This is Robert Altman's Bonnie and Clyde,if only to define the period and main ingredients, but it's very different. You can taste the dust of dried up roads and feel the desperation of the characters afloat in depression middle America. Altman's signature use of soundtracks is especially unique and effective in Thieves Like Us. In almost every scene, whether in a car on on a delapidated front porch, the radio is blaring in the background with the music or radio serials that define the time. It's a fantastic device.

Box of Moonlight/C,B-

Trilmark/1996/112m

     After seeing the wonderful Living in Oblivion, I had high expectations for Box of Moonlight. You might even say I was predisposed to liking it. Try as I might and try as director Tom DiCillo might, I was not able to warm to this comedy that struggles futilely for laughs and life observations.
     John Tutturro is faced with a life crisis. While on road assignment as a construction foreman, as the project is suddenly cancelled, he realizes that none of the workers like him, that he has lost the ability to enjoy himself. Realizing that he isn't too far from a lakeside resort he remembers from his childhood, he decides to return to that place of innocence to reassess his life. He finds the lake, but it's a memory poluted by time a chemicals.
     Everything changes, however, when John stops to help Jim on the road. Jim is the proverbial free spirit, a creature existing on the fringes searching for the pure joy of life. Traveller/B,B-

Hallmark/1997/101m

     When Traveller is charming, it works quite well and fortunately, that's most of the time. It takes a dark turn that is rather out of sorts with the rest of the movie, but the good nature of the film wins out over its faults.
     Bokky is a "traveller", part of a tribe of Irish decended Gypsies
     The first feature by Cinematograher Jack Green, I couldn't help but be somewhat disappointed by the nondescript look of the film. It's almost as if Green, as director, wanted to shy away from any fancy footwork with the camera that might detract from the simple nature of his story.
