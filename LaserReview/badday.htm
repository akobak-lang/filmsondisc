Bad Day at Black Rock/A, C+ 

 
US/1954/Color/Widescreen 2.28:1/Stereo/81 minutes/Directed by John Sturges/Starring Spencer Tracy/Robert Ryan/Voyager/19 Chaps/Theatrical Trailer/CLV/$49.95 

 From the opening shots of a train speeding through the desert accompanied by Andre Previn's rousing score,  Bad Day at Black Rock makes a  declaration of  the tension and excitement ahead. Directed by John Sturges,  this is one of the highlights of a directing career that spanned the years 1945 to 1977.  Boldly economical in style, though shot in color and Panavision, the film has a sense of tightly composed black and white images, though I do not mean to malign Sturges' full use of the wide screen. He emphasizes the barren vastness of  the terrain and the frightening isolation of the setting brilliantly. 

 The director has put together a cast working in exemplar ensemble fashion. Spencer Tracy is inspiring in the role of one-armed John J. MacReedy, a stranger on a straight forward mission to a small desert town who becomes embroiled  in the intrigue of the town's collective secret.  The dignity and power that Tracy packs into his broad beamed body is incredible. Tracy becomes a quintessential Western hero in this oater in an updated setting. Robert Ryan is a powerful force that leads the town against Tracy's intruder. There is never a doubt that Ryan means business. He is splendidly abetted by Ernest Borgnine and Lee Marvin as despicable town toughs.  Borgnine's comeuppance at the hand(sic)of Tracy is one the screen's most stirring fights and it is all the more remarkable for its incredible brevity, another reminder of the superlative prudence with which Sturges has crafted the film. It might be interesting to note that although Tracy soundly defeated Borgnine in the screen fight, he lost the fight for the Academy Award for Best Actor to Borgnine, who won against Tracy(also nominated for MacReedy) for his role in Marty. 
 
The disc is not up to what it could be. The pressing has an excessive amount of grain evident. It is not clear whether it emanates from the transfer or disc manufacture. The color palette is a trifle pale, but it does not work against the film unduly. There are a number of scenes in which the image is slightly soft. The digital sound is solid and powerful, though not extraordinary. The Criterion treatment does include an excellent audio commentary on the left analog track by John Sturges. Sturges is not shy about sharing secrets of the filmmaking process. The thinking that went into his decision making makes a fascinating accompaniment to the film. In addition to observations about the actors, Sturges unselfishly acknowledges credit for the success of the film to the strength of the  screenplay, and even comments that the opening sequence of the film was inspired by  criticism from a marketing executive at a rival studio. 
 
The dramatic potential for this film has been fully realized. It is a thoroughly satisfying film experience and it received my nomination for compulsory viewing by film enthusiasts. 
 MGM has recently released its own version of the film, sans the wonderful commentary by Sturges. It was not viewed for comparison, but might be worth a look for those not interested in Sturges' illumination.
