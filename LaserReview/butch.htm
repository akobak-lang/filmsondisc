Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid B, A-
US/1969/Color/Widescreen 2.35:1/Mono/110 minutes/Directed by George Roy Hill/Starring Paul Newman and Robert Redford/Fox/49 Chaps/Audio Commentary, Supplementals/2 discs/CLV/$99.98

Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid is a good natured film that makes great use of its assets, but in the end lives in a nether land somewhere between comedy and western. In the tradition of the western, it never generates enough excitement or tension to satisfy the purist, and in terms of the comedy, it works well because the comedy evolves naturally from the characters and situations, but it is certainly never a side-splitting laugher. It does have Paul Newman and Robert Redford at the height of their screen charms and they bring a dimension to the film that lesser acting chemistry could never have produced.

The production elements of the film are excellent. Director George Roy Hill is a skilled filmmaker who brings out the best in his players and collaborators. His strong visual style combines wonderfully with the Academy Award winning photography of Conrad Hall. The screenplay, however, is meandering and the pacing thoroughly inconsistent. Screenwriter William Goldman gives his characters many wonderful things to say, but much as I enjoyed the film, it really sags under the weight of the relentless posse.

The 25th Anniversary special edition includes a documentary on the making of the film made by Robert Crawford, who has also served as producer for a number of Hill films. There are video interviews with Paul Newman, Robert Redford, William Goldman, Katherine Ross and Burt Bacharach, second audio commentary from director Hill, director of photography Hall, and documentarian Crawford, as well as a few comments from songwriter Hal David, theatrical trailers, and some documents relating to the shaping of the screenplay.

Being a sucker for those behind the scenes shots that include camera set-ups and crew in the foreground of a scene, I found the making of documentary lots of fun. It lends a different perspective to the movie making process. Comparing the relatively pale, washed out images on the documentary and the freshly made transfer of the film for the laser serves to emphasize the beauty of the laser medium. The primary narration on the documentary is from Hill and was evidently made just prior to release of the film. Everything is very fresh in the director’s mind. He provides an enormous amount of technical detail disclosing how the movie was made. To achieve the washed out, desaturated look that he and cinematographer Hall wanted, many scenes were overexposed.

The last section of the disc, talking heads of Goldman, Newman, Redford, Ross, and Bacharach is enjoyable. These are just reminiscences and the principals tend to focus on a couple of stories rather than detailed impressions of what the movie making process was like, which makes the accompanying documentary all the more important. It was interesting to note that when Redford was talking about the negative critical reaction to the film, he infers that the extreme popularity of the film with audiences proved the critics wrong. The sad truth is, lots of critically lambasted and poorly made movies go on to whopping box office success, but that doesn’t make them good movies.

Most of the chores on the second audio are taken up by Conrad Hall and Robert Crawford, with a lesser participation from George Roy Hill. Listening to Hall speak is a particular joy. Seldom have I sensed such love of one’s work equal to that expressed by Conrad Hall. He reflects on the joys of physically operating the camera. He likes to close in on the eyes of his actors. Some of the best moments on the audio track are Hall’s "oohs & ahhhs regarding specific shots. It gives the viewer a chance to look back with the marvel of the creator of the image. There were a number of times when the "day for night" process was used and Hall several times expressed his hate for the process. While Hall laments the "day for night," Crawford provides sensible rational for its use in particular situations. The fact comes out that Newman couldn’t stand horses, which provides a retrospective laugh. And Hall speaks wistfully of his love affair with Katherine Ross. Hall looks for the accident to bring new dimension to his work. He loves the falling light of "magic hour" and tight cramped faces in the frame of the camera. The second audio is thoroughly enjoyable.

This is a really lovely transfer which reproduces the subtleties of cinematography that Hall sought to capture in Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid. Levels are consistent throughout and the delicacy of color just fine. The image is always sharp. The disc production is top notch. It’s encased in a handsome box featuring embossed gold lettering and a signature cameo of Butch, Sundance and Etta adorns the front. The insert includes chapter notes with excellent indications of the person who is speaking on the audio as well as the specific timings. This is an edition that is likely to sell out.
