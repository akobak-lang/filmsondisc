Comancheros, The/ B-, A- 

 
US/19961/Color/Widescreen 2.3:1/Stereo/107 minutes/Directed by Michael Curtiz/Starring John Wayne, Stuart Whitman, Ina Balin/Fox Video/44 Chaps/Theatrical Trailer/CLV/$59.98 
 
There is a pervasive exuberance that    propels The Comancheros beyond the limitations imposed by  minimal plotting. John Wayne's  typical late career screen image, a tough veneer leavened by a feisty sarcastic humor, effectively dominates the screen.  Wayne's Texas Ranger Jake Cutter is an appealing character and the actor balances his macho posturing well with an underlying streak of sentiment. Stuart Whitman clearly functions as support to  Wayne in the role of Monsieur Paul Regret, a New Orleans  dandy on the run from injustice. He works well with appealing Ina Balin in a dubiously constructed romance that serves to link together the chain of narrative. 

 Spells between the action drag somewhat, but the settings are colorful. Sequences that take place in the Comanchero stronghold are well done, but those moments involving the  Texas Rangers fall flat. Lee Marvin is fun as an distinctively  coifed gun runner, yet the scenes between he and Wayne are overdone. 
 
While the  widescreen  scenes are well filmed, they are not extraordinary. The music by Elmer Bernstein is outstanding, however, and significantly enhances the overall impact of the film.  Though the film is entertaining, there is a sense that individual aspects of the film add up to a sum greater than the whole. 
 
The disc production is a special collector's edition that features audio commentary on the left analog track from a quartet of the films actors, Whitman, Patrick Wayne, Nehemiah Persoff and Michael Ansara. There comments are not scene specific and take the form mostly of career anecdotes. Whitman claims that Wayne virtually took over direction from Curtiz about halfway through the film. Some of his observations are interesting, such as the fact that during the filming of The Longest Day the actors had to suck on ice cubes to prevent their breath from showing during the Winter filming of scenes depicting Summer. Michael Ansara's droning reminiscences are quite boring. A sampling of production stills, lobby cards and posters round out the extras on the disc. 
 
Fox has made a handsome production of The Comancheros. The transfer has been accomplished in fine fashion. Widescreen images are in sharp focus and the colorful setting has been rendered in accurate tones. There is no undue evidence of video artifacts and the pressing is very clean. The soundtrack is presented crisply.
