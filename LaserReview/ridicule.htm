Ridicule/B+, B

France/1995/Yellow English Subtitles/Color/Widescreen 2.35:1/Stereo Surround/103 minutes/Directed by Patrice Leconte/Starring Fanny Ardant, Charles Berling/Miramax/18 Chaps/CLV/$

Ridicule exudes sophistication. It presents a fascinating picture of the court of Louis XVI, the intrigue, jealousies and love affairs revolving in and around Versailles. Covering similar territory to Dangerous Liaisons, this period piece develops more naturally with more humanity.

Nobleman Ponceludon de Malavoy comes to the court of Louis XVI seeking funds for an irrigation project to rid his native province of swamp related maladies. The only way he can hope to reach the King’s ear is by attracting his attention is participating in the court’s ritual exchange of wit. Through a chance meeting with the Marquis de Bellegarde, a savvy member of the King’s court, Malavoy is tutored in the art of wit and court intrigue. A quick learn, he quickly makes his mark in court and attracts the attention of Madame de Blayac, a connected lady with a healthy appetite for sex. Along the way he falls in love with Bellegarde’s daughter, the virtuous Mathilde, conquers the court and gets his audience with Louis, suffers at the lash of court wit and loses his love. Fortunately, love conquers all and the French Revolution takes care of the rest.

Patrice Leconte, whose Monsieur Hire was an interesting study in obsessive perversion, composes wonderful images in for a totally different world in Ridicule. Combining the witty script with elegant photography, Leconte weaves an entertaining tale. Set design is fantastic. Costumes, buildings, gardens, even plates of food evoke the period with authenticity.

Charles Berling is a sincere Malavoy and Jean Rochfort brings a world weariness to Bellegarde. Fanny Ardant appears to be having a grand old time as the lascivious Madame de Blayac and fresh face Judith Godreche is a beauty as Mathilde.

It appears that low light interiors are the order of the day in Ridicule. They provide a natural look for the time period, but they also put a strain on the film stock. The result is a grainy film which transfers into a grainy laser disc. Grain is exacerbated on laser, but Ridicule achieves a reasonable balance between sharpness, light and grain. The yellow English subtitles are easy to read over the picture. Music from Antoine Duhamel is recorded with lovely transparency.
